## Not Written for a Wedding

If you've been to more than a handful of weddings, you've heard 1 Corinthians 13 read aloud -- usually in a soft, reverent tone, framed as the ultimate description of romantic love. But Paul didn't write this chapter for brides and grooms. He wrote it to a church in Corinth that was tearing itself apart with jealousy, favoritism, lawsuits between members, and arguments over whose spiritual gift mattered most.

The word Paul uses, translated "charity" in the King James (agape in Greek), isn't romantic affection. It's a deliberate, willed love -- love as action and choice, not just feeling. Paul sandwiches this chapter between two chapters about spiritual gifts because the Corinthians were using their gifts like weapons, competing over who was more impressive. Paul's answer: none of it means anything without love. You can speak in tongues of men and angels, but without love, you're just noise.

## Love as a List of Verbs

Notice how practical and unglamorous this list actually is. Suffereth long. Is kind. Envieth not. Vaunteth not itself. Is not puffed up. Is not easily provoked. These aren't abstract feelings -- they're behaviors, choices made in ordinary, often irritating moments. Paul isn't describing the rush of falling in love; he's describing the grind of staying patient with someone who's let you down for the third time this week.

This is why the passage applies to so much more than marriage. It applies to the coworker who takes credit for your work, the friend who's constantly late, the family member who always has to be right. Love, biblically defined, suffers long -- it doesn't have a short fuse. It isn't easily provoked -- meaning it absorbs offense without immediately firing back. It doesn't envy -- meaning it can genuinely celebrate someone else's success without quietly resenting it.

Read this way, 1 Corinthians 13 stops being a pretty poem and starts being a mirror. Most of us can tolerate the idea of love in theory. It's the specific, unglamorous practice of it -- not being puffed up, not keeping score, not reacting to provocation -- that actually tests whether we're living it out.

## Love in the Ordinary Friction

Here's the application: the next time you're annoyed, overlooked, or provoked today, let this list run through your mind, not as poetry, but as instruction. Is your response patient, or are you keeping a mental tally of wrongs? Are you quietly resentful of someone else's success, or genuinely glad for them?

Paul wrote this to a divided church because love was the thing that could hold fractured relationships together when doctrine and gifts and personal preference couldn't. The same is true now. Whether it's a marriage, a friendship, a workplace, or a church disagreement, this kind of love -- patient, kind, not self-seeking, not easily angered -- is still the thing that holds people together when everything else pulls them apart.

It's worth remembering, too, that this love isn't natural to us. Paul is describing a supernatural fruit, the kind the Spirit produces, not something we manufacture through sheer willpower. So when you fall short of it -- and you will -- that's an invitation to ask for help, not evidence that real love is unreachable. The God who is love Himself is the source of the patience and kindness this passage calls for.
