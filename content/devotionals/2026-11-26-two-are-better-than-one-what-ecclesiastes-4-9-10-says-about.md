## A Preacher Tired of Chasing the Wind

Ecclesiastes is one of the strangest books in the Bible -- an aging king, traditionally understood to be Solomon, cataloguing everything he tried in search of meaning: wealth, pleasure, work, wisdom itself, and finding most of it to be "vanity," or hevel in Hebrew, a word that literally means vapor or breath -- something that looks solid but dissolves the moment you try to hold it.

It's striking, then, that in the middle of all this disillusionment, chapter 4 lands on something the Preacher doesn't dismiss: companionship. Right before this passage, he describes a man who works endlessly, has no heir, no companion, and asks himself, "for whom do I labour?" (4:8). It's one of the bleakest moments in the book. And it's immediately followed by this almost tender counterpoint: two are better than one.

## The Math of Friendship

The Preacher doesn't argue for community with abstract sentiment -- he builds a case with concrete, almost mundane examples. If one falls, the other helps him up. If two lie down together, they keep warm (a real concern in a world without central heating, where travelers often shared body heat to survive cold nights). If someone attacks one, two can resist; a threefold cord isn't easily broken.

What's notable is that none of these examples describe glamorous friendship. They describe friendship doing ordinary, almost unremarkable work: showing up, generating warmth, offering a hand up after a stumble. This is the real substance of community -- not grand gestures, but the quiet reliability of someone else being there when things go wrong, which, the Preacher assumes, they eventually will.

## The Warning Hiding Inside the Promise

Verse 10 doesn't just praise companionship -- it names the specific danger of its absence: "woe to him that is alone when he falleth, and he hath no one to help him up." It's not a condemnation of being single or solitary by circumstance. It's a warning about isolation during hardship specifically -- the particular vulnerability of facing a fall with no one positioned to catch you.

This lands differently in a culture that often prizes independence as a virtue in itself. We praise people for not needing anyone, for handling things alone, for being self-sufficient. Ecclesiastes doesn't share that value. It assumes falling is inevitable for everyone, and it treats isolation in that moment not as strength but as genuine risk. Community isn't a nice supplement to a well-lived life in this passage -- it's part of what makes labor, and life, bearable at all.

If you've drifted from friendships lately -- let busyness, hurt, or just the slow erosion of time pull you away from people who used to know your life in detail -- this verse is worth sitting with honestly. Who would help you up if you fell this week? Who are you positioned to help up? Rebuilding community rarely happens through one dramatic reconnection; it happens the way the Preacher describes it -- through the unremarkable, repeated acts of showing up for each other, long before the fall ever comes, so that when it does, somebody's already close enough to reach you.

Bring people into my life I can lean on, and make me someone others can lean on too. Give me the courage to reach out instead of withdrawing. Build real community around me, Lord, not just acquaintance. Amen.
