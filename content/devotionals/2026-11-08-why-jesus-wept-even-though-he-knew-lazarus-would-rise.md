## The Shortest Verse With the Longest Shadow

John 11:35 is two words in English, three in Greek — *edakrysen ho Iesous*. It sits in the middle of one of the most dramatic resurrection scenes in Scripture, and that's what makes it so strange. Jesus is on His way to raise Lazarus from the dead. He has already told His disciples, "Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep" (John 11:11). He knows exactly how this story ends. And yet, standing at the tomb with Mary and the mourners, He weeps.

The Greek word used here, *dakryo*, is different from the louder, wailing grief described of Mary and the Jews just a few verses earlier (*klaio*, to wail or lament loudly). Jesus's weeping is quieter — tears streaming, not a public outcry. Some scholars think this distinction matters: Jesus isn't performing grief for the crowd. He's not weeping because He lacks faith about what's coming. He's weeping because death itself — the wrongness of it, the wreckage it leaves in people who loved someone — grieves Him. Grief and loss moved the Son of God to tears even when He held the cure in His own hands.

## What This Means When Your Own Loss Doesn't Make Sense

If you've walked through grief and loss, you know the strange guilt that can creep in — the sense that if you really trusted God, you wouldn't cry so hard, or so often, or so long after the funeral. John 11:35 dismantles that lie. Jesus believed completely in the resurrection, and He still wept at a grave. Knowing the ending doesn't cancel the pain of the middle. Faith was never meant to be a tear-stopper; it was always meant to be a hand to hold while the tears fall.

Notice too where Jesus places Himself in this moment: not above the mourners, instructing them to calm down, but among them, undone by the same loss they feel. The Bible doesn't present a God who manages grief from a safe distance. It presents a God who stands at the tomb and lets it cost Him something. That's the God available to you today, whether you're grieving a parent, a marriage, a child, a friendship, or a version of your life that didn't survive.

## Letting Tears Be Part of the Story, Not a Detour From It

There's a temptation, especially in church culture, to rush grief toward resolution — to get to the Lazarus-walks-out-of-the-tomb part as fast as possible. But the text doesn't rush. Jesus pauses. He asks where Lazarus was laid. He groans in His spirit (John 11:33). He weeps. Only after all of that does He move toward the miracle. The order matters: sorrow isn't skipped on the way to hope; it's walked through.

If you're carrying grief and loss right now, let this verse do something practical for you today: stop apologizing for your tears. Stop timing your sorrow like it has an expiration date other people are watching. Jesus didn't. He let the loss be real before He did anything about it, and so can you. Tell someone the actual weight of what you're carrying instead of the polished version. Let yourself cry in front of God without narrating why it's taking so long. He already wept at the very grave He was about to empty — He's not waiting for you to stop grieving before He moves close.

And when the ache comes back at 2 a.m., or at a song, or at an empty chair during the holidays, remember this isn't evidence that your faith has failed. It's evidence that you loved something real enough to miss it — and that the God who wept at Lazarus's tomb is not surprised, or disappointed, by your tears today.

I bring You the loss I'm carrying right now, the tears I've held back or apologized for. Meet me here the way You met Mary and Martha — not with lectures, but with Your presence. Help me believe that hope and sorrow can live in the same moment. In Jesus's name, Amen.
