## A Song Written for Exiles Who Had Lost Everything

Psalm 147 was likely composed after the Israelites returned from Babylonian exile, a season when the nation was grieving generations of loss — homes destroyed, loved ones buried in foreign soil, a temple reduced to rubble. Verse 3 sits right in the middle of a psalm celebrating God rebuilding Jerusalem and gathering the outcasts of Israel. This isn't abstract poetry written by someone untouched by sorrow; it's a promise spoken directly into a community carrying real, communal grief and loss.

The Hebrew word translated "broken" here is *shabar*, which means shattered or crushed — the same word used for breaking pottery or fracturing bones. And "bindeth up" (*chabash*) is a medical term, the same action a physician takes when wrapping a wound to help it heal properly. The psalmist isn't offering vague comfort; he's describing God as a skilled healer who binds wounds with intention and care, not one who simply tells the wounded to move on.

## Why Grief Doesn't Disqualify You From God's Presence

One of the quiet lies grief tells us is that our sorrow is somehow a spiritual failure — that if we trusted God enough, we wouldn't hurt this much. But Psalm 147:3 assumes brokenness as the starting point. God doesn't heal the broken-hearted, are healed. It doesn't say He heals those who have already stopped grieving. He meets people exactly where the grief and loss have left them: shattered, wounded, still bleeding on the inside.

This matters because grief rarely follows a tidy timeline. You might feel steady for weeks and then be undone by a smell, a song, or an empty chair at the table. The psalmist's God isn't rattled by that unpredictability. He specializes in binding up wounds that reopen, in returning again and again to tend to what's broken rather than demanding you heal on schedule.

## Living This Out When the Ache Feels Permanent

If you're walking through grief and loss right now, let this verse reshape two things: how you see God, and how you treat yourself.

First, resist the urge to hide your grief from God, as though He needs you to perform strength before He'll respond. The whole point of this verse is that He moves toward brokenness, not away from it. Bring Him the specific wound — the empty side of the bed, the phone number you can't delete, the anniversary you're dreading — and ask Him to bind it up, even if healing comes slowly and in stages.

Second, give yourself permission to still be in process. Bones don't heal the moment they're set in a cast; they need time, protection, and often a slow rebuilding of strength around the injury. Grief works similarly. Surround yourself with people who will sit with you rather than rush you, and look for small, tangible ways God is binding up what's broken — a friend who calls at the right moment, an unexpected wave of peace during a hard anniversary, a verse that suddenly makes sense in a new way.

Psalm 147 goes on to describe a God who counts the stars and calls them each by name (v. 4), then immediately turns to say He heals the brokenhearted (v. 3). The juxtaposition is deliberate: the same God who sustains galaxies is personally, specifically attentive to your grief. Your loss is not too small for His notice, and it's not too big for His healing. He is, even now, binding up what has been broken in you.
