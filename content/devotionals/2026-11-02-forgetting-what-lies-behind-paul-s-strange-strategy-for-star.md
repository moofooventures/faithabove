## A Man With Plenty to Remember

It's worth pausing on who's saying this. Paul wasn't someone with a clean slate asking you to imagine a fresh start was easy. By the time he wrote Philippians, he had a past that included actively persecuting Christians, holding the coats of men who stoned Stephen to death, and dragging believers from their homes to be imprisoned. He also had a past full of religious credentials he once considered impressive—trained under a top rabbi, a Pharisee, blameless by the law's standards (he lists these just a few verses earlier). In other words, Paul had both a past to be ashamed of and a past to be proud of, and he treats them almost identically: both get left behind.

The phrase 'forgetting those things which are behind' doesn't mean Paul had amnesia about his history. The Greek word *epilanthanomai* suggests something closer to no longer being controlled or defined by it—releasing its grip rather than erasing the memory. Paul knew exactly what he'd done. He just refused to let it set the terms for what came next.

## The Posture of Reaching

The image Paul uses next is athletic, likely drawn from the footraces common in Greek culture that his Philippian readers would have recognized immediately. 'Reaching forth' translates a word used for a runner leaning his whole body toward the finish line, eyes fixed ahead, arms extended—someone who has stopped looking over his shoulder because looking back would only slow him down. You cannot sprint effectively while twisted around to check your starting line.

This is a strikingly practical picture of what a new beginning actually requires. It's not usually one clean break where the past simply vanishes. It's a continual posture, a daily leaning forward, choosing again and again not to let yesterday's failures or yesterday's successes determine today's direction. Paul says this 'one thing I do'—present tense, ongoing. Starting over wasn't a single decision for him; it was a repeated discipline.

## Living Forward From Here

If you're facing a new beginning right now—a fresh start after a failure, a major life transition, a relationship ended, a new season you didn't expect to be standing in—this passage offers something more useful than vague encouragement. It offers a strategy: name what's behind you honestly, then deliberately stop letting it write your next chapter.

That might mean refusing to replay the mistake that still embarrasses you every time it surfaces in your mind. It might mean not clinging to a former version of yourself—the job title, the relationship, the way things used to be—as though reaching for something new is a betrayal of who you were. Paul had reason to cling to his former status and reason to be paralyzed by his former sins, and he let go of both for the same reason: neither one was the prize. The 'high calling' ahead of him mattered more than the ground already covered.

New beginnings rarely come with a guarantee that the past won't ache anymore. But Paul isn't asking the Philippians to feel nothing about their history. He's asking them to keep moving despite it. Whatever is behind you today, you're allowed to leave it there and reach for what's ahead with both hands.

PRAYER: Lord, thank You that my past—the parts I regret and the parts I'm tempted to be proud of—doesn't have to determine where I go next. Help me release the grip of what's behind me without pretending it didn't happen. Give me the posture Paul describes, leaning forward instead of looking back, even when moving on feels difficult. Thank You for the high calling You've set in front of me. Help me press toward it one day at a time. In Jesus' name, amen.
