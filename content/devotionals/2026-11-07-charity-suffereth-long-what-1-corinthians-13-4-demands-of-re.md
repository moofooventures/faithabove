## A Love Poem Written to Stop a Fight

It's easy to forget that 1 Corinthians 13 wasn't written for weddings. Paul wrote it to a church in Corinth that was tearing itself apart—factions forming around favorite teachers, lawsuits between believers, spiritual gifts used to show off rather than serve. Chapter 13 sits right in the middle of a long discussion about those gifts, almost like Paul stops mid-argument to say: none of this matters without love.

The word Paul uses, translated "charity" in the King James, is *agape*—a Greek word that doesn't describe affection or attraction but a deliberate, willed commitment to someone's good regardless of what they've done to deserve it. This wasn't romantic love. It was corrective love, aimed at a fractured congregation that needed to hear what real love actually requires of people who'd rather be right than kind.

## Suffereth Long: Love That Outlasts Provocation

The phrase "suffereth long" translates *makrothymia*, a word built from "long" and "temper" or "passion." It describes patience specifically in the face of people who are provoking you—not patience waiting in a slow checkout line, but patience with someone who has wronged you repeatedly and shows no sign of stopping. This is love's first and hardest requirement: the willingness to stay engaged and good-willed toward someone even when every instinct says to retaliate or walk away.

In relationships—marriages, friendships, families, churches—this kind of love gets tested constantly. It's one thing to love someone who's easy to love. It's another to keep choosing their good when they've hurt you and haven't earned another chance. Paul puts this quality first because without it, nothing else about love holds together.

## Kind, Not Envious, Not Puffed Up

The rest of verse 4 reads almost like a checklist, and that's intentional. "Kind" (*chresteuomai*) is the only positive action word in a string of negatives, and it's notable that it's a verb, not an adjective—love doesn't just feel kind, it does kind things actively and consistently. Then Paul names what love refuses to do: envy, which eats at relationships through comparison and resentment; and being "puffed up," a term he uses elsewhere in this very letter to describe the inflated self-importance causing all of Corinth's division in the first place.

It's worth noticing that Paul is describing love almost entirely through behavior, not emotion. Nowhere does he say love feels a certain way. He says love does and doesn't do certain things. That reframes love as something you can choose on a hard day even when the feeling isn't cooperating—which is good news, because feelings are unreliable, but choices are available to you right now.

## What This Looks Like Today

If you're in a strained marriage, a tense friendship, or a family relationship worn thin by years of hurt, this verse isn't offering sentiment—it's offering instruction. Love suffers long means you don't bail at the first sign of difficulty. Love is kind means you actively look for ways to serve the person, even when you don't feel like it. Love doesn't envy or boast means you stop measuring your relationship against someone else's highlight reel. Real love, the kind Paul describes, isn't about how you feel toward someone this morning. It's about what you're willing to do for their good, day after day, whether or not they deserve it yet.

PRAYER: Lord, show me where I've confused love with a feeling instead of a daily choice. Give me patience to suffer long with the people who provoke me most, especially those I live with and love the longest. Teach me to be kind in action, not just in sentiment, and to let go of envy and pride that quietly poison my relationships. Help me love the way You've loved me—steadily, patiently, without keeping score. Make my love look like Yours today.
