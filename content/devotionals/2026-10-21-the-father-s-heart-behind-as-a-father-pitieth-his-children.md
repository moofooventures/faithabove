## An Unexpected Word: Pity

The English word "pitieth" can sound almost condescending to modern ears, like looking down on someone. But the Hebrew word here, racham, carries none of that coldness. It's related to the word for womb (rechem) and conveys a deep, gut-level, tender compassion — the kind that moves a parent to act, not just feel sorry from a distance. David, writing this psalm, chose a word that describes visceral love, the kind a mother feels toward the child she carried, applied here to a father's heart toward his children. This is compassion with muscle behind it.

David had every reason to know both sides of fatherhood intimately — his own complicated relationship with his father Jesse, who didn't even think to call him in from the fields when Samuel came looking for Israel's next king (1 Samuel 16), and his own deeply painful record as a father to Absalom and other sons. Yet here, David reaches past broken human fatherhood to describe something steadier: how the Lord himself relates to "them that fear him." The imperfections of earthly parenting don't disqualify the metaphor — they make the contrast with God's perfect fatherhood even more striking.

## What This Means for the Weary Parent

If you're in the thick of parenting right now — sleepless nights, teenage silence, the guilt of snapping when you meant to be patient — this verse offers something important. It doesn't ask you to be a perfect father or mother in order to reflect God's heart. It invites you to look at your own compassion for your children, however flawed, and understand that it's a dim reflection of something far more complete in God toward you. When you feel compassion well up for your child despite their failures, their messes, their attitude — that feeling, multiplied infinitely and purified of all your own selfishness and fatigue, is how God feels toward you.

This also reframes parenting itself. Psalm 103:13 isn't a parenting instruction manual, but it quietly models something parents need: pity that doesn't shame. God's compassion toward "them that fear him" isn't contingent on them getting everything right. It's rooted in relationship — in the fact that they're his. In the same way, children don't need parents who demand performance before compassion. They need a safe place to fail and still be pitied, held, pursued.

## Fearing Him, Not Fearing Punishment

The phrase "them that fear him" is key and easy to misread. Biblical "fear of the Lord" isn't terror of punishment; it's reverent awe, a right-sized posture before a holy God, the kind that leads to trust rather than hiding. The verses right before this one (103:11-12) describe how far God removes our transgressions from us — as far as the east is from the west. So the compassion described in verse 13 comes immediately after a description of forgiveness. God's pity isn't despite our failures; in some sense it's wrapped around them, surrounding people who come to him honestly rather than those who pretend to have it together.

For parents today, this is worth modeling. Children who fear consequences learn to hide. Children who have a right, warm reverence for a parent who forgives quickly and loves steadily learn to come close when they've messed up, not run further away. If parenting has felt like a performance review in your home — yours or your child's — this verse offers a gentler pattern. Compassion first. Correction within relationship, not instead of it. And underneath all of it, the quiet reminder that your own Father in heaven pities you the same way, even on the days you've failed as a parent yourself.

PRAYER: Father, thank you for loving me the way a good father loves his children — not with distant disappointment, but with deep, moving compassion. Forgive me for the moments I've parented out of fear or control instead of out of the gentle love you've shown me. Help me extend to my own children the same patient compassion you extend to me, even when I don't deserve it. Teach me to fear you rightly, with reverence that draws me close instead of pushing me away. Thank you for removing my failures as far as the east is from the west. Amen.
