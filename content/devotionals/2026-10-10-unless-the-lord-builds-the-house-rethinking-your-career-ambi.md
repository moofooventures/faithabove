## A Psalm Attributed to Solomon, the Builder King

This psalm carries the superscription 'A Song of degrees for Solomon'—and the irony is rich, because Solomon was arguably the greatest builder in Israel's history. He constructed the temple, palaces, store cities, and fortified entire regions (1 Kings 9). If anyone had reason to trust in his own labor and expertise, it was Solomon. Yet this psalm, likely written by or for him, opens by declaring that all that building is vanity unless the Lord is the one truly building it.

The psalm uses two vivid images from everyday ancient life: a builder constructing a house, and a watchman guarding a city through the night. Both represent the two basic human efforts to secure a stable life—shelter and safety. Houses required enormous labor to build in the ancient world; city watchmen stood exhausting, dangerous night shifts to protect against raiders and invaders. These weren't symbolic labors—they were literal, backbreaking work. And the psalm says: all of it is in vain without the Lord's hand in it.

## The Bread of Sorrows

Verse 2 gets even more pointed, naming the specific temptation: rising early, staying up late, eating 'the bread of sorrows'—an evocative Hebrew phrase describing the exhausting, anxious labor of someone trying to secure their own future through sheer effort and worry. This isn't a condemnation of hard work itself; Scripture elsewhere praises diligence (Proverbs 6:6-11). It's a condemnation of a particular posture toward work—one driven by anxious self-reliance rather than trust.

This distinction matters enormously for how we think about our careers today. Ambition itself isn't the problem. The problem is when our work and career become an attempt to build security apart from God, fueled by exhausting over-function, sleepless striving, and the quiet belief that our effort alone determines our outcome.

## Working Without the Anxious Grip

The psalm's closing line is tender: 'for so he giveth his sleep to his beloved.' God's beloved ones can actually rest, because their security was never resting on their own shoulders in the first place. This doesn't mean careless or lazy work—Solomon himself worked tirelessly. It means working from a position of trust rather than anxious striving.

Practically, this reshapes how we approach our careers. It's worth asking: am I working hard because I trust God with the outcome, or am I working anxiously because I believe the outcome depends entirely on me? Are my long hours an act of diligence, or are they 'bread of sorrows'—exhausting labor fueled by fear rather than faith?

This doesn't mean abandoning effort or ambition in your work and career. It means inviting God into the building rather than building alone, and resisting the cultural pressure to equate busyness with faithfulness. The watchman who trusts the Lord can actually close his eyes for a few hours. The builder who trusts the Lord can leave the results in bigger hands than his own. That's not passivity—it's the kind of confidence that frees you to work diligently without being crushed by the weight of carrying your whole future alone.

PRAYER: Lord, I confess how often I've tried to build my career and security on my own strength, carrying anxious weight that was never mine to carry. Teach me to work diligently without striving anxiously. Help me trust that you are building what matters, even when I can't see the whole structure yet. Give me rest instead of the bread of sorrows. Thank you for being present in my work, not just waiting at the end of it. Amen.
