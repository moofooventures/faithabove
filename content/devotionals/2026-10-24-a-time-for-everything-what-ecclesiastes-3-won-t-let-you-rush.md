## The Preacher's Long List

Ecclesiastes 3 opens with one of the most famous verses in the Old Testament, followed immediately by fourteen paired opposites: a time to be born and a time to die, a time to plant and a time to pluck up, a time to weep and a time to laugh. The author — traditionally identified as Solomon, writing as "Qoheleth" or "the Preacher" — had tested every form of wisdom, pleasure, and achievement available to a king and found all of it, on its own, to be "vanity," or hevel in Hebrew, meaning vapor or breath. Something that can't be grasped or held still.

That word hevel is key to understanding why verse 1 counts as wisdom rather than resignation. Solomon isn't saying life is meaningless; he's saying life is uncontrollable, and real wisdom starts with accepting that truth rather than fighting it. The list of opposites that follows isn't random — it's structured as merisms, a Hebrew literary device where two extremes represent the whole spectrum in between. "A time to plant and a time to pluck up" doesn't just mean farming; it covers every human effort that has a beginning and an ending, a rhythm you don't fully control.

## Wisdom Means Knowing What Season You're In

Much of human frustration comes from trying to force the wrong season — demanding harvest before you've planted, insisting on laughter while you're still in a season meant for weeping, or trying to build when it's actually time to tear down and start fresh. The wisdom of Ecclesiastes 3 is deeply practical precisely because it refuses to hand you a formula. It doesn't say which season you're in; it says seasons exist, they have their own timing, and discernment — wisdom — is learning to recognize where you actually stand instead of where you wish you were.

This matters because the book of Ecclesiastes was likely written for a court audience, people with resources and power who were used to controlling outcomes. Solomon, of all people, had the means to try to manufacture every good season on demand — and his conclusion, after trying, was that it couldn't be done. Wisdom, then, isn't mastery over timing; it's humility before it. James 1:5 promises that wisdom is given generously to those who ask, and this is often exactly what we need wisdom for: discerning what time it actually is in our lives, rather than what time we wish it were.

## Living Inside Your Actual Season

If you're in a season of loss, Ecclesiastes doesn't shame you for not being further along. If you're in a season that calls for building, it doesn't let you coast on rest you haven't earned yet. The wisdom here is an invitation to stop resisting the actual shape of this chapter of your life and instead ask God what it's for. A season of silence might be a season to listen. A season of waiting might be a season to prepare. A season of mourning, per verse 4, is allowed to be exactly that, without rushing toward the "time to dance."

Practically, this week, try naming your current season honestly in prayer — not the season you think you should be in, but the one you're actually living. Ask for wisdom not to escape it, but to walk faithfully within it, trusting the One who set the times and holds the seasons you can't control.
