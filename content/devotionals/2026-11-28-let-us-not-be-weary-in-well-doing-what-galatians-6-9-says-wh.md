## A Farming Verse for People Who Aren't Farmers

Paul wrote Galatians to a church being pulled apart by false teachers insisting Gentile believers needed to adopt Jewish law to be truly saved. It's a letter full of theological argument, but it ends, as his letters often do, with practical instruction for ordinary life — and this verse is agricultural because almost everyone reading it understood farming. You plant seed in one season and harvest in another. There's a long, unglamorous middle where nothing visible happens except waiting, weeding, and wondering if the seed took at all.

The word Paul uses for "weary" (*ekkakeo*) means to lose heart, to give up from exhaustion — it's different from physical tiredness. You can be rested and still weary in this sense; it's a fatigue of the will, not the body. And "faint" in the second half, a related word, pictures someone collapsing before the finish line, not from injury but from depletion. Paul is naming a very specific danger: not opposition, not failure, but the slow erosion of resolve that happens when effort keeps going out and nothing visible comes back.

## The Gap Between Planting and Harvest

This verse doesn't promise there won't be a gap. It assumes one. "In due season" is doing a lot of work in this sentence — it means the harvest comes on its own timeline, not yours, and that timeline is frequently longer than you'd choose. Perseverance, biblically, isn't about ignoring that gap or pretending it isn't painful. It's about continuing to do the next right thing inside the gap, even when the field looks exactly the way it did last month.

This is where a lot of us quit — not because we've concluded the work doesn't matter, but because we can't see proof that it's working. The parent praying for a wayward child for years. The person showing up to recovery meetings with no dramatic breakthrough yet. The employee doing excellent work nobody seems to notice. The friend who keeps reaching out to someone who rarely reaches back. Paul isn't writing to people in crisis moments — he's writing to people in the long middle, where perseverance is less about heroics and more about simply not stopping.

## What Keeps You From Fainting

Notice the conditional clause at the end: "if we faint not." The harvest isn't in question — Paul states it as a certainty, "we shall reap." What's in question is whether we'll still be standing when it arrives. That reframes the whole struggle. The real battle of perseverance isn't whether God will be faithful to the outcome; it's whether you'll still be in the field when the outcome shows up.

Practically, that means perseverance needs fuel that isn't visible results. Paul gives a clue just one verse earlier and one verse later — he talks about sowing to the Spirit rather than the flesh, and doing good "especially unto them who are of the household of faith." In other words: keep your eyes on the kind of seed you're planting and who it's for, not on how fast it's growing. Check in with people further down the road than you who can testify that the harvest really does come. Rest when you're depleted rather than pushing until you collapse — fainting, after all, is what Paul is trying to prevent, not glorify.

If you're in a season where perseverance feels like it's costing more than it's returning, this verse isn't asking you to feel energized about it. It's asking you to keep showing up to the field one more season, trusting a due season you can't yet see, because the God who set the harvest in motion hasn't changed his timeline just because you're tired.

PRAYER: Father, I'm tired in a way that rest alone doesn't seem to fix. I've been doing what's right for a long time without seeing the harvest, and I don't know how much longer I can keep going like this. Help me not to faint before the due season arrives. Remind me that the waiting doesn't mean the seed failed. Give me strength for today only, not the whole harvest season at once, and help me trust your timing more than my impatience. Amen.
