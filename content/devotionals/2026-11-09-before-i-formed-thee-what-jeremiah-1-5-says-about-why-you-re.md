## A Word Before He Was Ready

Jeremiah didn't ask for this job. When God spoke this word over him, he was likely a teenager, untrained, and terrified of public speaking (he says as much two verses later: "I cannot speak: for I am a child"). Yet God's declaration doesn't wait for Jeremiah's readiness. It reaches backward, past his birth, past his first breath, into the womb itself. The calling wasn't assigned after God saw what Jeremiah could do. It was placed on him before he could do anything at all.

This is easy to read as poetic language about God's foreknowledge, and it is that. But it's also a statement about purpose and identity that the original audience would have heard as shocking. In the ancient Near East, your place in the world was usually determined by birth order, tribe, and family trade. Jeremiah came from a priestly line in Anathoth, likely expecting a quiet, predictable life. Instead, God tells him his real assignment predates all of that. His calling isn't an accident of circumstance -- it's something woven into him before circumstance existed.

## Purpose Isn't Something You Build From Scratch

We tend to think of calling as a thing we discover through trial and error, or construct through enough self-improvement. Jeremiah's story flips that. The word used for "knew" here (yada) implies intimate, relational knowledge, not just intellectual awareness. God didn't merely foresee Jeremiah's existence; He knew him the way you know someone you love. The calling flows out of that knowing, not the other way around.

This matters for anyone wrestling with purpose today, especially if your path feels late, uncertain, or nothing like you imagined at twenty. Your sense of calling isn't something you're racing to catch up to. If God's knowledge of you preceded your first breath, then your worth and your purpose were never contingent on your resume, your platform, or your current season of clarity. The call was already there when you had nothing to offer it.

## What This Looks Like on a Tuesday

Jeremiah's calling wasn't abstract -- it was specific and immediately costly. He was sent to confront kings, deliver warnings nobody wanted to hear, and watch his nation fall apart around him. Purpose, biblically, rarely means an easy, flattering assignment. It means being positioned where you're needed, even when you feel unqualified.

Practically, this means your calling probably isn't hiding in some dramatic, singular revelation you're waiting to receive. It's more likely already visible in what you've been given to steward: the people in your home, the skills you've quietly built, the burdens that won't leave you alone. Ask what keeps surfacing in your life that feels both heavy and strangely yours to carry. That tension -- unwanted but undeniable -- is often exactly where calling lives.

If you're in a season where your purpose feels foggy, Jeremiah's story offers a strange comfort: the fog doesn't mean the calling isn't there. It means you're standing where Jeremiah stood, young and unsure, being told that the plan long preceded the panic. The task now isn't to manufacture a purpose from nothing. It's to trust the One who already knew you before you were anything at all, and to take the next faithful step He puts in front of you.

PRAYER: Lord, thank You that You knew me before I had anything to offer You. When I feel unsure of my purpose, remind me that my calling was never dependent on my readiness. Help me trust that You are still forming me for what You've already planned. Give me courage to take the next step You've placed in front of me, even when I feel unqualified. Settle my heart in the truth that You have known me all along. Amen.
