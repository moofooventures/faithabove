## An Ancient Poem About Time

Ecclesiastes was written by "the Preacher" (traditionally understood to be Solomon, or a wisdom teacher writing in his voice) during a period when Israel's wisdom literature wrestled openly with life's hardest questions. Unlike the tidy proverbs of quick cause-and-effect, Ecclesiastes stares straight at the confusion of human experience — the repetition, the labor, the losses that don't make sense — and refuses to look away. Chapter 3 opens with one of the most quoted passages in all of Scripture: a list of fourteen paired opposites, each introduced by "a time." In Hebrew, the word for "time" here is *et*, which doesn't mean a fixed hour on a clock but a fitting season, an appointed moment that belongs to a larger rhythm.

What's striking is that grief gets its own line item, right alongside joy. "A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance." The Preacher doesn't rank these as good and bad, spiritual and unspiritual. He simply says: this too has its place. If you are walking through grief and loss right now, this verse is a quiet permission slip. You are not failing at faith by mourning. You are living inside the season God himself named.

## Why Grief Doesn't Need an Apology

One of the heaviest burdens for grieving people is the sense that they should be further along, that too many tears mean too little trust. Ecclesiastes 3 pushes back against that pressure. The Preacher lists mourning next to laughing, weeping next to dancing, and doesn't rush from one to the other. There's no verse fifteen that says "but hurry up and get to the dancing part." The list simply lets each season stand.

This matters because grief rarely follows a straight line. Some mornings feel almost ordinary, and then a smell, a song, or an empty chair at the table pulls you right back under. Ecclesiastes doesn't treat that as backsliding. It treats it as being human in a world east of Eden, where death interrupts what love built. The same God who numbers your days (Psalm 90:12) also numbers your tears — Psalm 56:8 says he keeps them in a bottle. Your grief is not an inconvenience to God. It is something he sees closely enough to record.

## Living Inside the Season Without Getting Stuck There

The hope tucked into this passage is structural: seasons change. The very grammar of the verse — "a time to weep, *and* a time to laugh" — assumes movement. Grief is real, but Ecclesiastes doesn't promise it's permanent. Just a few verses later, the Preacher writes that God "hath made every thing beautiful in his time" (v.11), suggesting that even the mourning season, painful as it is, gets folded into something God is shaping across the whole of a life, even when we can't see the pattern from inside it.

Practically, this means a few things for today. First, let yourself actually grieve instead of performing composure for others. Cry the tears that are due; write the letter you never sent; say the person's name out loud. Second, resist comparing your timeline to anyone else's — Ecclesiastes doesn't give a schedule, just an acknowledgment that this season is real and it is yours. Third, watch for the small, unforced moments when laughter sneaks back in, a shared memory that makes you smile instead of ache. That isn't disloyalty to your loss; it's the next season arriving quietly, the way seasons do.

If you're searching for bible verses for grief today, let Ecclesiastes 3 be the first stop rather than an ending point. It doesn't erase the pain, but it tells you the truth: your mourning has a place in God's ordered world, and so, in time, will your comfort.

When I'm tempted to rush myself or judge my own sorrow, remind me that you have made everything beautiful in your time, even what feels unbearable right now.
