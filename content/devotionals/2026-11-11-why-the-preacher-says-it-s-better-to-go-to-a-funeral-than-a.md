## A Verse That Argues With Us

Nobody wants to hear that mourning is 'better' than feasting. We spend enormous energy avoiding the house of mourning—skipping funerals when we can, changing the subject when grief comes up, rushing people back to normal. But the Preacher in Ecclesiastes, writing from a long life of watching kings rise and fall, vineyards planted and uprooted, isn't being morbid for its own sake. He's making an observation about what actually shapes a person.

The Hebrew word translated 'house of mourning' (beit ebel) referred to the structured period of communal grief in ancient Israel—neighbors gathering, meals brought, formal weeping that could last seven days or more. It wasn't private or quiet. Grief in that culture was witnessed. And Qoheleth's point is that standing in that house, face to face with 'the end of all men,' does something a party never can: it makes the heart honest.

## What Sorrow Teaches That Comfort Can't

Verse 3 is even harder to swallow: 'sorrow is better than laughter... by the sadness of the countenance the heart is made better.' This isn't a vote against joy—Ecclesiastes elsewhere tells us to eat our bread with gladness. It's a recognition that grief and loss strip away illusion. At a feast, we can pretend we're invincible, that our plans will all come to pass, that time is unlimited. At a funeral, none of that holds. The heart gets recalibrated toward what's real and what's temporary.

Anyone who has sat with real grief and loss knows this isn't poetic exaggeration. The death of a parent, a marriage, a friendship, a season of life—these losses carve out space in us that laughter alone never reaches. They force the question laughter lets us dodge: what actually matters here? C.S. Lewis wrote after his wife's death that grief felt like fear, like waiting—but also like clarity he hadn't expected. The sadness of the countenance, strange as it sounds, can make the heart better: more honest, more tender, less distracted by what doesn't last.

## Living in the House of Mourning

This doesn't mean seeking out grief or romanticizing sadness. It means not fleeing the house of mourning when life brings you to its door—your own or someone else's. So many people try to rush grief and loss into a tidy resolution, pressuring themselves or others to 'move on' within a respectable window. Ecclesiastes suggests the opposite: stay in the house long enough to let it teach you something. Let the living 'lay it to his heart'—let mortality do its quiet work of reordering your priorities.

Practically, this might mean not changing the subject when a grieving friend brings up their loss. It might mean allowing yourself to actually feel the weight of a loss instead of numbing it with busyness or forced positivity. It might mean visiting the cemetery, keeping the photograph out, telling the stories. None of this is despair—it's formation. The heart made better by sorrow is a heart that has stopped pretending life is a feast that never ends, and has started living like every day with the people we love is a gift with an expiration date.

Grief and loss will come to every house eventually. Ecclesiastes doesn't promise to remove that reality—it promises that leaning into it, rather than around it, makes us wiser, softer, and more awake to what matters.

## A Word for Today

If you're in a season of grief and loss right now, you are not doing it wrong by feeling the weight of it. The Preacher would tell you that your sadness is doing something in you that a thousand distractions couldn't. Let your heart be made better, even here.

PRAYER: Lord, I don't want to rush past my grief just to feel comfortable again. Teach me what this sorrow has to show me about what truly matters. Help me sit with loss honestly instead of running from it, and soften my heart rather than letting it harden. Thank You that You are near to the brokenhearted, even in the house of mourning. In Jesus' name, Amen.
