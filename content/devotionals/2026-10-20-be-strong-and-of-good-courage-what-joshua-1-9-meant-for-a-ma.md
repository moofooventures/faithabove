## Stepping Into Someone Else's Shoes

Joshua had spent forty years as Moses' assistant, watching one of the greatest leaders in Israel's history guide a nation through impossible circumstances. Now Moses was dead, and Joshua was being handed the job—leading over two million people into a land filled with fortified cities and hostile armies. It's no wonder God's charge to him opens with repetition: "be strong," "be of good courage," "be not afraid," "be not dismayed." Four commands stacked on top of each other, because Joshua needed to hear it more than once.

This verse comes at the very start of the book that bears his name, right after God has already said something similar twice in the preceding verses. By verse 9, God is essentially repeating Himself a third time. That repetition isn't filler—it's a signal that courage rarely comes from hearing something once. It has to be reinforced, returned to, remembered.

## Courage Grounded in Presence, Not Personality

Notice what the courage is based on. God doesn't tell Joshua he's naturally brave, or that he has what it takes, or that his track record proves he's ready. The command to be courageous is grounded entirely in a promise: "for the Lord thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest." The Hebrew word for courage here, 'amats, means to be alert, physically strong, and firm—not the absence of fear but the decision to move forward despite it.

This is a crucial distinction for anyone today who equates courage with confidence. Joshua likely didn't feel confident. He had watched the previous generation die in the wilderness because of fear and unbelief at the very borders of this same land (Numbers 14). He knew the stakes of hesitation. His courage wasn't self-generated; it was borrowed from the certainty of God's presence going with him.

## When You're the One Taking Over

Maybe you're stepping into a role you didn't ask for—caring for aging parents, leading a team after someone left, raising kids alone, rebuilding after a loss. The command to have courage can feel almost insulting when you're this tired and this uncertain. But look again at what God says right before the charge: "Have not I commanded thee?" It's not a suggestion offered gently. It's an authoritative reminder that courage is required, but it's required of someone who is not alone.

The cultural backdrop matters here too. Ancient Near Eastern treaties and royal commissioning ceremonies often included formulas like this—a king or leader would be publicly charged with strength and assured of the support of a greater power as he took office. God is essentially commissioning Joshua the way a sovereign would commission a vassal king, except the greater power here isn't a human empire. It's the God who parted seas and brought down walls.

## Practicing Courage Today

Courage, biblically, is less a feeling you wait for and more a decision you make with trembling hands. You can say the words "be strong and of good courage" over a situation you're dreading this week—before the hard conversation, the diagnosis appointment, the first day of something new—not because you've suddenly stopped being afraid, but because you're choosing to move forward anyway, trusting that God goes with you into it.

Whatever your version of entering the land looks like today, the same ground holds true: you are not required to have courage on your own strength. You're invited to borrow His.
