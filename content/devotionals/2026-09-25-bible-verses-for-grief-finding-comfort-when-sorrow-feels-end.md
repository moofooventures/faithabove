## A Blessing That Doesn't Make Sense at First

When you're in the thick of grief, the word "blessed" can feel almost offensive. How can mourning be a blessing? Jesus opens the Sermon on the Mount with a list of statements that turn the world's values upside down, and this one sits right in the middle of them. The Greek word translated "blessed" here is *makarios*, which doesn't mean happy in a shallow, circumstantial way. It points to a deep, settled state of being that comes from being rightly aligned with God, regardless of what's happening around you. Jesus isn't saying grief feels good. He's saying that those who grieve are not abandoned by God — they are, in fact, positioned to receive something the comfortable and numb often miss.

In first-century Jewish culture, mourning was communal and expected to be visible. People tore their garments, sat in ashes, wailed publicly for days. There was no pressure to "move on quickly" or grieve in private. Jesus' original audience would have understood mourning as a natural, even necessary, response to loss — not something to suppress. That cultural backdrop matters, because so much of our modern grief and loss experience comes wrapped in pressure to appear "fine" within weeks. Scripture never asks that of us.

## Comfort Is a Promise, Not a Maybe

Notice the structure of the verse: mourning now, comfort later, but comfort guaranteed. The Greek verb for "comforted" is *parakaleo*, the same root behind the title given to the Holy Spirit — the Comforter, the one called alongside. This isn't a vague, someday-things-will-feel-better sentiment. It's a specific promise that God himself draws near to those grieving, in the present tense as well as the future one. Psalm 34:18 says the Lord is near the brokenhearted, and Matthew 5:4 tells us that nearness produces real comfort, not just distraction from pain.

This matters because grief and loss often make people feel like their sorrow is a spiritual failure — as if enough faith would erase the tears. But Jesus wept at Lazarus's tomb even knowing he would raise him moments later (John 11:35). Grief is not a lack of faith. It's love that has nowhere to go, and God meets it directly rather than asking us to bypass it.

## Living This Out Today

If you're walking through loss right now — a parent, a spouse, a child, a friendship, a season of life that's ended — let yourself actually mourn instead of rushing toward composure. Talk to God honestly about what hurts, the way the Psalms do again and again. Grief and loss are not tidy processes with a finish line; they come in waves, sometimes years apart, triggered by a song or a smell or an empty chair at the table. Each wave is not evidence that you're stuck. It's evidence that you loved deeply.

Practically, this might mean giving yourself permission to cry without apology, journaling prayers instead of just thinking them, or reaching out to someone who has walked through similar loss and won't rush your process. It might mean revisiting old photos on hard anniversaries instead of avoiding them. Comfort from God often comes through people — a meal left on the porch, a friend who simply sits with you in silence, a pastor who remembers the date your loved one passed.

When you feel like your grief has no end in sight, hold on to the shape of this verse: mourning is named first, but it is not the final word. Comfort follows. It may not erase the loss, but it will meet you inside it, steady and real, exactly where you are today.

PRAYER: Lord, I bring you my grief today, even the parts I don't fully understand myself. Thank you for not asking me to pretend I'm fine when I'm not. Draw near to me the way your word promises, and let your comfort meet me in the middle of this sorrow. Help me to grieve honestly instead of rushing past it. Remind me that love and loss are tangled together, and that you see every tear. In Jesus' name, amen.
