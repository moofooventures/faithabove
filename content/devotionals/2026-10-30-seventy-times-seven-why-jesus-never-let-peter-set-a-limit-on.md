## A Reasonable Question From a Reasonable Man

Peter wasn't being stingy when he asked about forgiveness. In first-century Jewish tradition, rabbis taught that a person should forgive someone three times for the same offense — a standard drawn loosely from Amos 1-2, where God pronounces judgment on nations "for three transgressions... and for four." Peter doubled that number and added one for good measure. Seven times would have sounded almost extravagant to the disciples listening. He expected Jesus to commend his generosity.

Instead Jesus answered with a number that isn't really a number at all. "Seventy times seven" (some translations render it "seventy-seven") isn't a ceiling to reach and then stop. It's Jesus deliberately breaking the math. You can't keep a tally that large in your head during a real conflict — and that's the point. The moment forgiveness becomes something you count, it's no longer forgiveness. It's bookkeeping.

## The Parable That Follows

Jesus didn't leave it there. He told the story of a servant forgiven an impossible debt — ten thousand talents, more money than a laborer could earn in several lifetimes — who then turned around and choked a fellow servant over a few months' wages. The master's fury in that story isn't really about the smaller debt. It's about the servant's failure to let what he'd received reshape how he treated others. Forgiveness, in Jesus' math, isn't a transaction between equals. It's a response to having been forgiven a debt you could never repay.

This is where forgiveness gets hard in real life, not theoretical life. Maybe it's a parent who never said sorry. A spouse whose words still echo. A friend whose betrayal you replay before falling asleep. The temptation is to treat forgiveness like a limited resource you dole out carefully, making sure the other person has "earned" enough of your mercy. Jesus says the opposite: forgiveness flows out of what you've already been given, not out of what the other person deserves.

## What Forgiveness Is Not

It helps to say what this passage doesn't mean. Forgiveness isn't pretending the wound didn't happen. It isn't staying in danger or allowing ongoing abuse. It isn't even necessarily reconciliation — sometimes a relationship can't safely be rebuilt, even after forgiveness is extended. What Jesus is describing is a release: letting go of your right to make someone pay for what they did, because you've already been released from a debt infinitely larger.

This is a daily, sometimes hourly, choice rather than a single decision made once. The hurt doesn't always leave when you decide to forgive — sometimes you'll have to make the same choice again tomorrow, and the day after. That's not failure; that's what "seventy times seven" actually looks like lived out. It's not a one-time act of heroic grace. It's a posture you keep returning to, especially toward the people closest to you, where old wounds get reopened the easiest.

## Living It Out Today

Is there someone whose name makes your stomach tighten? Someone you've silently kept score against? Jesus isn't asking you to minimize what happened. He's asking you to remember how much you've been forgiven, and to let that memory loosen your grip on what you're owed. Start small if you need to — pray for the person by name, even if the words feel hollow at first. Forgiveness often begins as an act of will before it becomes an act of the heart. Give it time, and give it today.
