## A Psalm Written for People Who Had Given Up

Psalm 107 reads almost like a collection of rescue stories stitched together — wanderers lost in the desert, prisoners in chains, sailors tossed by storms, and here, in verses 17-20, people suffering "because of their transgressions" who cried out to God "in their trouble." This particular group is described as fools who "afflicted themselves" and whose "soul abhorred all manner of meat" — they'd lost their appetite for life itself, sick in body and spirit, drawing near death's gates.

Then comes verse 20: "He sent his word, and healed them." Not a spell, not a formula, not even a direct touch described in the text — His word. In Hebrew thought, God's word was never just information; it was active, living, and able to accomplish what it declared. This is the same concept behind Genesis 1, where God speaks and creation responds. When the psalmist says healing came through God's word, he's describing the same creative power that made the world now being aimed at restoring broken people.

## Healing That Starts Before the Symptoms Change

What's striking about this psalm is the order of events. The people cried out first — before anything changed physically. Verse 19 says, "Then they cried unto the LORD in their trouble, and he saved them out of their distresses." The healing followed the cry, not the other way around. This flips how we often approach healing today, where we wait to feel better before we feel able to pray, or wait for proof that God is listening before we trust Him with our pain.

This psalm suggests healing is often preceded by honest desperation, not polished faith. These people weren't righteous when they cried out — the text says their trouble was partly self-inflicted. And God sent His word anyway. That's worth sitting with if you've convinced yourself you need to have it together before bringing your body, your diagnosis, your depression, or your addiction to God.

## What It Means to Receive His Word Today

We don't experience God's spoken word the way these ancient people might have — through a prophet's declaration or a felt sense of divine intervention. But we do have Scripture itself, which the New Testament calls "living and active" (Hebrews 4:12), and we have Christ, whom John describes as the Word made flesh. For believers today, healing often comes through a combination most of culture resists holding together: real trust in God's power to heal, paired with real use of the doctors, medicine, counselors, and time God also provides.

If you're waiting on healing right now — physical, emotional, relational — this verse doesn't promise instant results on your timeline. It promises that God's word, once sent, accomplishes what it's sent to do. Isaiah 55:11 echoes this exact idea: God's word "shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please." Healing in Scripture is rarely described as random. It's described as sent — purposeful, directed, intentional toward you specifically.

So bring your actual condition to God today, however messy or self-inflicted it feels. Read His word not as a magic incantation but as the ongoing voice of the God who specializes in sending exactly what broken, desperate people need. The same God who healed the wanderers in Psalm 107 hasn't run out of words to send.

PRAYER: Lord, I bring my need for healing to You today, whatever shape it takes in my body, my mind, or my relationships. Thank You that Your word has power to heal and restore, even when I've contributed to my own trouble. I cry out to You now, not because I have it together, but because I need You. Send Your word into the places I feel closest to giving up. I trust Your timing even when I can't see the outcome yet. In Jesus' name, Amen.
