## A Letter to People Who Had Every Reason to Quarrel

Paul wrote Colossians to a church he'd never visited, planted by his friend Epaphras in a city known for trade routes, mixed cultures, and the kind of close quarters that breed friction. The word he uses for "quarrel" here is the Greek *momphe* -- it literally means a grievance or complaint, the kind of thing you'd file in a legal dispute. Paul assumes his readers have real grounds for resentment. He doesn't say "if anyone imagines a problem." He says if any man *have* a quarrel -- a legitimate one.

That's worth sitting with. Forgiveness in Scripture was never meant for imaginary offenses. It's for the real debt, the real betrayal, the real wound that still has a name attached to it.

## Why 'Forbearing' Comes First

Notice the order of Paul's sentence: forbearing, then forgiving. Forbearance is the daily, ongoing choice to put up with someone's flaws before any formal act of forgiveness is even required. It's the slow, unglamorous work of not keeping score yet. Only after that does Paul move to forgiveness proper -- the releasing of a debt once it's been named.

This two-step pattern matters because forgiveness isn't usually one dramatic moment. It's forbearance repeated daily, and then forgiveness extended when the moment calls for it. If you're waiting to feel ready for the big release, start smaller: practice the forbearing. Let today's small irritation go without filing it away for later use.

## 'Even As Christ Forgave You'

The measuring stick Paul gives isn't "forgive as much as feels fair." It's "forgive as Christ forgave you." That phrase would have landed hard on Colossian ears, because the forgiveness of Christ wasn't cheap or theoretical -- it cost him everything, and it was offered before anyone asked for it or deserved it. Paul isn't asking the Colossians to forgive from a place of strength or moral superiority. He's asking them to forgive the way they themselves were forgiven: undeservedly, fully, and before the other person cleaned up their act.

This is where forgiveness gets hard in real life. We want the other person to earn it first -- an apology, an admission, some visible change. But the Christian pattern runs the other direction. The forgiveness comes first, sometimes long before any repair in the relationship. That doesn't mean reconciliation is automatic or that trust is rebuilt overnight. Forgiveness and reconciliation are not the same thing. You can release someone from the debt they owe you without handing them back every privilege of the relationship.

## What This Looks Like on an Ordinary Tuesday

Most of us aren't being asked to forgive a single catastrophic betrayal today. We're being asked to forgive the same small thing again -- the sibling who never calls, the coworker who takes credit, the parent whose words still sting twenty years later. Unforgiveness rarely announces itself as rage; it shows up as a cold rehearsal of the offense, replayed in the shower or during a commute.

If there's a name surfacing in your mind right now as you read this, that's probably not an accident. Try naming the actual quarrel, the real debt, honestly -- not minimizing it, not exaggerating it. Then ask what it would look like to release that one person from what they owe you, today, regardless of whether they ever ask. You may need to do this again tomorrow. That's not failure; that's what forbearing one another actually looks like in practice.

PRAYER: Lord, there's a name I keep bringing back up even when I say I've let it go. Thank You for forgiving me long before I deserved it or even asked. Help me forbear the small things today without keeping score, and give me the strength to release the larger debts I'm still holding onto. I don't want bitterness to have the final word in my heart.
