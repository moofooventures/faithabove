## A Crowd in Tears

The scene behind Nehemiah 8:10 is easy to miss if you only read the verse itself. The exiles had just returned to Jerusalem, rebuilt the broken walls, and gathered to hear the Law of Moses read aloud for the first time in a generation. Most of them had never heard it. As Ezra read and the Levites explained the meaning, the people began to weep—openly, loudly, in front of the whole assembly. They were grieving their history of disobedience and the decades of exile it had cost them.

It's into that grief that Nehemiah, Ezra, and the Levites say something startling: stop crying. Go eat rich food, drink something sweet, and share with those who have nothing prepared. This is a holy day, they say, and 'the joy of the LORD is your strength.' Joy isn't being offered as a replacement for honest sorrow—it's being offered as the appropriate response to what God had just done in bringing them home and restoring His word to them.

## Joy as Fuel, Not Feeling

The Hebrew word for strength here, *ma'oz*, carries the sense of a stronghold or fortress—something you retreat into, something that holds you up when everything around you is unstable. That's a strange partner for joy, which we usually think of as a light, fleeting emotion. But Nehemiah isn't describing a feeling that floats on top of circumstances. He's describing a fortified confidence rooted in who God is and what He has done, available even when the circumstances are a half-rebuilt city and a people just realizing how far they'd drifted.

This is the kind of joy Scripture keeps pointing to—not manufactured cheerfulness, but a settled trust that produces real strength. James tells believers to 'count it all joy' when they face trials (James 1:2), not because trials are pleasant, but because joy anchored in God survives what joy anchored in good news cannot. The joy of the Lord doesn't wait for your circumstances to improve before it shows up; it's available the moment you remember whose you are.

## What This Looks Like Today

Most of us treat joy as something we'll get to once the hard part is over—once the diagnosis clears, the finances stabilize, the relationship heals. But Nehemiah's audience was standing in the rubble of their own history, still rebuilding, still grieving, when they were told joy was available to them right then. That's instructive. Joy in the biblical sense isn't the absence of sorrow; it can sit right alongside it, the way it did for the people that day, who wept and feasted within the same hours.

Practically, this means you don't have to wait for a 'better' season to choose gratitude, celebration, or generosity. The text's instruction to share food with 'them for whom nothing is prepared' matters here too—joy was meant to be communal, not hoarded. If you're in a season where joy feels hard to locate, start where they did: remember what God has done, gather with others, and let shared gladness do some of the lifting your own strength can't.

If you're running on empty today, it's worth asking whether you've been looking for strength everywhere except the one source Nehemiah points to. The joy of the Lord isn't a bonus emotion for when life is easy. It's meant to be the thing that holds you up when it isn't.
