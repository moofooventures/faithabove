## A Command Repeated Three Times

By the time God speaks this verse to Joshua, he's already been told to be strong and courageous twice in the same chapter. That repetition isn't accidental or redundant — it's emphasis. Joshua was standing at the edge of an enormous transition: Moses, the only leader Israel had known for forty years, had just died, and Joshua was left to lead a whole nation into a land filled with fortified cities and formidable enemies. If anyone had reason to feel underqualified and afraid, it was him.

What's notable is that God doesn't deny the danger ahead. He doesn't tell Joshua the battles will be easy or that the Canaanite armies are weaker than they look. Instead, God grounds Joshua's courage in something entirely outside the circumstances: His own presence. "For the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest." The courage isn't rooted in Joshua's military skill or the size of his army — it's rooted in the promise of accompaniment.

## Courage Isn't the Absence of Fear

The phrasing "be not afraid, neither be thou dismayed" is instructive. God is not suggesting Joshua feels nothing. He's addressing real, reasonable fear head-on. Biblical courage was never about the absence of fear; it's about refusing to let fear have the final word. Joshua likely did feel afraid — anyone facing what he faced would. The command wasn't "don't feel fear," but rather "don't let it dismay you, don't let it paralyze you into disobedience or retreat."

This distinction matters enormously for how we understand courage today. If you're waiting to feel fearless before you take the next step — into a hard conversation, a new job, a diagnosis, a move — you may be waiting for something that never fully arrives. Courage, biblically speaking, is acting rightly in spite of fear, not after it's gone.

## "Have Not I Commanded Thee?"

The verse opens with a question that almost sounds like a rebuke, but it's really a reminder. God is pointing back to His own authority and His own word as the basis for Joshua's confidence. It's as if He's saying: this command didn't come from your own confidence in yourself — it came from Me, and I don't issue commands I won't back up. Joshua could draw courage not from self-belief but from the reliability of the One who spoke.

That's a crucial shift for us too. So often we try to manufacture courage internally, searching for enough self-assurance to move forward. But real, sustainable courage in Scripture is almost always externally sourced — rooted in the character and presence of God rather than in our own resolve, which can waver and fail.

## Walking Into Your Own Jordan River

You may not be facing walled cities, but you likely have your own version of the unknown ahead of you — a decision, a diagnosis, a change you didn't choose, a responsibility that feels too large. Joshua's story reminds us that courage isn't reserved for the naturally fearless. It's given to ordinary people who choose to move forward anyway, trusting they are not doing it alone. Whatever you're facing today, you're not required to feel brave first. You're invited to take the step, trusting the same presence that steadied Joshua is available to steady you.
