## A Strange Verse to Find in a Book About Meaninglessness

Ecclesiastes spends most of its pages chasing down every source of meaning under the sun — pleasure, wealth, wisdom, legacy — and watching each one crumble into "vanity," or hevel, a Hebrew word that literally means vapor or breath. So it's almost jarring to land on 9:10, a verse that sounds like it belongs in a modern productivity book: whatever your hand finds to do, do it with all your might. Solomon, the traditional author, isn't contradicting himself. He's arriving somewhere important. If life under the sun is fleeting and you can't control outcomes, wealth, or how long you'll live, the only sane response left is to throw yourself fully into the work actually in front of you, today, with the strength you have right now.

The phrase "whatsoever thy hand findeth" is deliberately broad. It's not limited to prestigious callings or dream jobs. It includes the mundane task, the thankless assignment, the work nobody will remember by next year. Solomon isn't grading the work by its glamour. He's grading your engagement with it.

## Why 'There Is No Work in the Grave' Changes the Math

The second half of the verse is the theological engine behind the first. Solomon reminds us that the grave ends all opportunity for work, planning, learning, and wisdom as we know it now. In the ancient Near Eastern worldview, Sheol was a shadowy place of diminished activity — not annihilation, but a cessation of earthly labor and striving. Solomon uses this grim fact not to produce despair but urgency. Because this life is the only window you get to work, to contribute, to build, to serve — don't waste it in half-hearted shuffling.

This has real teeth for how we think about work and career today. So many of us treat our jobs as something to merely survive — count down to Friday, do the minimum, save the real effort for hobbies or weekends. Solomon isn't saying work is the point of life; he's already dismantled that idea elsewhere in Ecclesiastes. But he is saying that while you're there, present and able, disengagement is a waste of the one life you've been given. The Teacher isn't preaching hustle culture or burnout. He's preaching presence — full engagement with the task at hand, because the window for that kind of engagement is shorter than you think.

## Doing It With Your Might, Not With Your Anxiety

For the Christian, Paul adds another layer centuries later in Colossians 3:23: "whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men." This takes Solomon's urgency and gives it a target. Your work isn't ultimately for your boss, your resume, or your own sense of accomplishment — it's offered to God, which means even unseen, unglamorous work carries weight and dignity.

Practically, this might mean approaching Monday differently. Instead of resenting the task in front of you or performing it on autopilot, do it with your might — with attention, with care, with the understanding that this ordinary hour is part of the finite, meaningful life you've been given. It might mean letting go of comparing your career to someone else's, and instead fully showing up for the work your actual hand has found today. Not because your job defines your worth, but because full-hearted work, done as an offering, is one of the quiet ways we honor the life God handed us — brief, vaporous, and real.
