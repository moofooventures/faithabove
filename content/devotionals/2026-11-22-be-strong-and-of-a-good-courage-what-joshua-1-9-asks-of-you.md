## Spoken to a Man Who Had Every Reason to Be Afraid

God said this to Joshua at one of the worst possible moments to feel confident. Moses — the only leader Israel had ever known — had just died. Joshua, his successor, had to lead over a million people into a land filled with fortified cities and hostile nations, starting with Jericho. He wasn't a seasoned king stepping into an easy transition. He was an aide stepping into impossible shoes, and God's first words to him, repeated three times in this chapter, are some version of "be strong and courageous."

That repetition matters. God doesn't say it once and move on. He says it again in verse 7, and again here in verse 9, because fear doesn't dissolve after a single pep talk. Courage, in the Hebrew (chazaq, amats), isn't the absence of fear — it's a command to act with strength and resolve regardless of what you feel. God isn't asking Joshua to stop being afraid. He's commanding him to move forward anyway.

## Courage Is Commanded, Not Just Felt

We tend to think of courage as a feeling that shows up before we act — like we need to feel brave first. But notice the structure of the verse: "Have not I commanded thee?" God frames courage as obedience, not emotion. That's freeing, actually. It means you don't have to wait until fear leaves to do the right thing. You can be afraid and still be obedient. You can feel dismayed and still take the next step.

This is especially relevant because the word "dismayed" in Hebrew (chathath) carries the sense of being shattered or broken down by terror — the kind of fear that makes you want to quit entirely. God names that specific feeling and tells Joshua not to let it have the final word. Not because the danger isn't real — Jericho's walls were real, the Canaanite armies were real — but because God's presence outweighs the threat.

## Why the Presence of God Is the Actual Source of Courage

The reason given for courage isn't Joshua's training, Israel's numbers, or a guaranteed easy victory. It's one phrase: "for the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest." That's the whole foundation. Courage in Scripture is almost never self-generated; it's borrowed from the character and presence of God. Joshua could face Jericho not because he'd become fearless, but because he wasn't facing it alone.

That's good news for whatever you're facing today — a diagnosis, a decision, a conversation you've been avoiding, a new season you didn't choose. Courage doesn't require you to feel ready. It requires you to take the next step while trusting that God hasn't sent you out alone. Ask yourself what you've been avoiding because you're waiting to feel brave enough. Then do what Joshua did: move forward with the fear still present, trusting the presence that goes with you is stronger than what's in front of you.

Jericho's walls eventually fell. But before they did, Joshua had to walk toward them while still, very possibly, afraid.
