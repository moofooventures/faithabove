## A Proverb, Not a Formula

Proverbs 22:6 gets quoted at baby dedications, hung on nursery walls, and recited like a contract: raise your kid right, and they'll turn out right, guaranteed. But Proverbs, as a genre, was never written as a book of guarantees. It's wisdom literature -- general truths about how life usually works, not iron-clad promises that override human free will. Right alongside this verse sits others like "the righteous shall never be removed" (10:30), statements that describe the normal pattern of a well-ordered life, not a magic formula that eliminates every exception.

That matters enormously for parents carrying guilt over a child who's strayed, or parents just starting out and terrified of getting it wrong. This verse was never meant to put the entire outcome of a child's soul on a parent's shoulders. It was meant to describe a pattern worth pursuing, not a vending-machine guarantee.

## In the Way He Should Go

The Hebrew phrase translated "in the way he should go" is more interesting than the English suggests. It's literally "according to his way" -- al-pi darko -- which carries the sense of according to his own bent, his own nature, the particular path suited to this particular child. This isn't a verse about forcing every child into an identical mold of behavior. It's closer to the old idea of apprenticeship: training a child according to who they actually are, their temperament, their gifts, the shape of the person God made them to be.

That reframes parenting significantly. It's not simply about transmitting a fixed set of rules uniformly to every child in the house. It's about paying attention -- noticing this child's particular wiring, and training within and toward that, rather than against it. Solomon, who likely compiled this proverb, had many sons by many wives, and not all of them turned out well (Adonijah's rebellion in 1 Kings 1 is a sobering footnote to a man famous for wisdom). Even the source of this proverb watched it play out imperfectly, which should soften how we read its promise.

## When He Is Old

The phrase "when he is old" is also worth slowing down on. It doesn't say "when he is twenty" or "when he leaves for college." Hebrew wisdom literature often takes the long view, and this proverb seems to as well. Many parents have watched a child drift far from everything they were taught in their twenties, only to see that same adult child return to it decades later -- the training resurfacing after years of silence, like a seed that stayed dormant through a long winter.

That doesn't erase the pain of watching a child walk away right now. But it does offer something sturdier than a guarantee with no exceptions: a long-game confidence that faithful training plants something real, even when the fruit takes far longer to show than we'd like. Parenting, especially in the hard seasons, often feels like throwing seed into soil you can't see beneath the surface. This proverb doesn't promise you'll see the harvest on your schedule. It promises the seed matters.

So if you're in the thick of parenting right now -- sleepless, second-guessing every decision, watching a teenager pull away, or grieving a grown child who's wandered -- this verse isn't a verdict on your effort. It's an invitation to keep training according to who this child actually is, trusting that the way you're pointing them doesn't vanish just because the road gets long. Parenting was never meant to be done by formula. It was meant to be done in faith, one ordinary day at a time, trusting the One who's holding the outcome you can't control.
