## A Song Written on the Far Side of Terror

Exodus 15 isn't a devotional written in a quiet garden. It's a song Moses and the Israelites sang with wet sand still on their feet, walls of water having just collapsed behind them, Pharaoh's army gone. Hours earlier they'd been trapped — sea in front, chariots closing in behind. There was no strategy that got them out. There was only God splitting the water and them walking through.

That's the context for "the Lord is my strength." It isn't a slogan for people who feel strong. It's the testimony of people who had nothing left and watched God become the strength they didn't have. The Hebrew word for strength here, oz, carries the idea of fierce, settled power — not a boost of adrenaline, but a foundation. Moses isn't saying God gave him strength. He's saying God IS his strength, the way a wall is load-bearing. Remove it and everything collapses; keep it and everything stands.

## Strength You Borrow, Not Strength You Build

We tend to think of strength as something we build up over time — willpower, grit, resilience we earn through repetition. Scripture keeps pointing somewhere else. The Israelites didn't get stronger overnight between Egypt and the Red Sea. They got weaker, more terrified, more ready to quit. Their strength came from outside themselves, at the exact moment they ran out of their own.

This matters because so much of the strength culture around us says dig deeper, push harder, find it within. And sometimes you can't. Sometimes you're standing at your own sea with chariots behind you and nothing within you but fear. Exodus 15:2 says that's precisely the moment to look up, not in. The song came after the miracle, but the trust came before it — Moses called God his strength while they were still walking between the walls of water, not knowing yet how the story ended.

## What This Looks Like on a Tuesday

Few of us will face literal armies or parted seas. Our Red Sea moments look like diagnosis results, custody hearings, a layoff notice, a child who won't speak to us. In those moments, strength in Christ doesn't usually arrive as a surge of confidence. It arrives as the ability to take one more step when you were sure you couldn't. It's the phone call you make for help instead of white-knuckling it alone. It's getting out of bed on the morning after the funeral.

The second half of the verse matters too: "he is become my salvation." Strength and salvation are tied together in this song — God's strength isn't abstract power, it's rescuing power, aimed at bringing His people somewhere safe. If you're exhausted today, the invitation isn't to try harder. It's to let God be your strength the way He was Israel's — not a cheerleader on the sidelines but the one doing the actual carrying.

Moses ends the verse with worship: "I will prepare him an habitation... I will exalt him." Strength received becomes strength offered back in praise. That's the pattern — we don't generate strength to prove something to God. We receive it and it turns, almost automatically, into worship, because we know exactly where it came from.

If you feel weak today, you're in good company with the people this verse was written for. They weren't singing from a place of power. They were singing from the beach, still shaking, still amazed they'd made it through. That's allowed to be your strength story too.

I give You my weakness today and ask You to become my song on the other side of it. In Jesus' name, Amen.
