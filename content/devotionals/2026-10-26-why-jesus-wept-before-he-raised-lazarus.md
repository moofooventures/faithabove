## The Shortest Verse, the Deepest Truth

John 11:35 is famous for being the shortest verse in the English Bible, but its brevity hides something enormous. Jesus is standing at the tomb of His friend Lazarus. He already knows He's about to raise him from the dead -- He said as much minutes earlier to Martha. And yet, knowing the resurrection is seconds away, He weeps anyway.

The Greek word used here, *edakrysen*, is different from the louder, wailing grief (*klaio*) used elsewhere in the same chapter to describe Mary and the mourners. Jesus's weeping is quieter, more personal -- tears welling up and spilling over. It's not performance grief. It's the real thing. This detail matters because it tells us something we often forget: grief and loss are not evidence of weak faith. The God who holds resurrection power in His hands still cries at a graveside.

## Grief Doesn't Need an Explanation to Be Honored

What's striking is that Jesus never explains His tears. He doesn't say "don't worry, I'll fix this in a minute" to make the sorrow smaller. He simply enters the pain of the people He loves -- Mary, Martha, the gathered mourners -- and lets Himself feel the weight of death's intrusion into a broken world, even death He's about to undo.

This is a comfort for anyone walking through grief and loss today. You may know, intellectually, that there is resurrection hope on the other side of your sorrow. You may even trust the promises of eternity with everything in you. But that knowledge doesn't erase the very real, very human need to grieve what's gone. Jesus modeled this. He didn't skip the tears to get to the miracle faster. The tomb scene teaches us that sorrow and hope can occupy the same moment.

The onlookers' response is telling too: "Behold how he loved him!" They read His tears correctly -- not as doubt, but as love. Grief, rightly understood, is love with nowhere left to go. If you are crying over someone you've lost, your tears are not a failure of faith. They are the evidence of how deeply you loved.

## Living Between the Tomb and the Resurrection

Most of us live in that same space Mary and Martha occupied -- grieving a real loss while trusting a God who has already defeated death, even if the full restoration hasn't arrived yet. That tension is uncomfortable, but it's not unfaithful. It's the normal Christian experience of grief and loss: lament now, hope secured for later.

Practically, this means you don't have to rush your grieving process to prove your trust in God. Let yourself weep at the places that hurt -- the empty chair, the unanswered phone call, the anniversary dates. Jesus didn't hurry past His own sorrow, and He doesn't ask you to hurry past yours either. At the same time, let the tears sit alongside hope, not instead of it. Lazarus did not stay in the tomb. The God who wept at that grave is the same God who called out, "Lazarus, come forth," and the grave had to obey.

If you are grieving today, bring your whole heart to Him -- the tears and the questions and the ache. He's not waiting for you to compose yourself before He'll sit with you. He already sat down in the dirt beside a grave once, and He'll sit with you in yours.
