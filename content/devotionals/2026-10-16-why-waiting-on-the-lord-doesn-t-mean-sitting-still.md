## A Prophet Speaking to Exhausted People

Isaiah wrote these words to Israelites living in exile, worn down by decades of displacement and broken promises. They weren't impatient in the modern sense of wanting faster Wi-Fi or quicker replies to a text. They were depleted—watching years pass with no clear end to their captivity, wondering if God had simply moved on without them. Into that weariness, Isaiah doesn't offer a pep talk about trying harder. He offers an image: eagles.

The Hebrew word translated "wait" here is *qavah*, and it's richer than our English sense of killing time. It carries the idea of being bound together, like strands of a rope twisted tightly around each other. To wait on the Lord wasn't to sit idle; it was to be tethered to Him, drawing strength from that connection while the waiting continued. Patience, biblically, was never about passivity—it was about posture. Where are you anchored while you wait?

## Eagles Don't Flap to Stay Up

Ancient listeners would have known something modern readers often miss: eagles don't climb by frantically beating their wings. They catch thermals—rising columns of warm air—and let the current lift them higher with barely any effort of their own. Isaiah's audience, living in a region where eagles and vultures were common sights riding desert updrafts, would have pictured this immediately. The strength Isaiah promises isn't the strength of exertion; it's the strength of being carried.

That distinction matters if you're in a season of patience right now—waiting for healing, a job offer, a reconciled relationship, a child to come home, an answer to years of prayer. Patience doesn't mean forcing your own momentum. It means staying tethered to God long enough to catch what He's lifting you with. The runners don't faint and the walkers don't grow weary, Isaiah says—notice the order moves from soaring to running to simply walking. Even the ordinary, plodding parts of waiting are covered by this promise.

## Practicing Patience Today

Patience gets a bad reputation because we treat it like a holding pattern before real life resumes. But Scripture consistently ties patience to renewal, not stagnation. If you're waiting on something right now, ask what it would look like to stay tethered rather than just tolerating the delay. That might mean continuing to show up in prayer even when answers feel distant, or choosing to trust God's timing over your own timeline, or simply refusing to let the wait convince you that God has forgotten you.

The exiles reading Isaiah's words didn't get an instant return home. Their waiting lasted generations. But the promise wasn't that the wait would be short—it was that the waiting itself could be sustained by something greater than their own willpower. That's still true. Your patience isn't measured by how calmly you endure delay, but by who you're holding onto while you do.

If today feels like one long wait with no eagle-wings in sight, remember that even walking without fainting is listed as part of the promise. You don't have to soar every day to be living out patience well. You just have to stay tethered.
