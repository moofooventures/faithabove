## A Surprisingly Practical Book Makes a Case for Friendship

Ecclesiastes is one of the more unexpected places to find encouragement about community, mostly because the whole book reads like the journal of a man who's grown suspicious of almost everything — wealth, pleasure, achievement, even wisdom itself. "The Preacher" (traditionally Solomon) spends twelve chapters circling the question of what actually holds up under the weight of a meaningless-feeling life. And right in the middle of that search, he stops to say something strikingly unambiguous: two are better than one.

The context right before this verse is a man working himself to exhaustion with "neither child nor brother," toiling alone with no end to his labor and no one to share it with (4:8). It's a portrait of isolated striving — the kind many of us know well, whether it's working long hours with no support system, carrying parenting or caregiving largely solo, or simply drifting through adulthood without close friendship. The Preacher names that isolation as a kind of vanity, an emptiness, even when the labor itself is successful.

## The Image of Falling and Lifting

The specific picture Ecclesiastes uses — falling and being lifted up — would have resonated with an agrarian, often treacherous landscape: rocky terrain, uneven roads, physical labor that put bodies at real risk. Falling wasn't metaphorical for most of the original audience; it was a literal occupational hazard. If you fell while traveling alone, you might lie there injured with no one to help, possibly for a dangerous length of time. If you fell with a companion, you were back on your feet quickly.

The Preacher uses that everyday reality to make a spiritual and emotional point: community exists not just for companionship in good times but for rescue in bad ones. Friendship isn't a nice supplement to a good life — it's often the mechanism by which you survive the hard parts of it. The verses that follow extend the metaphor further (warmth from lying together, strength in a three-stranded cord), but this falling-and-lifting image is the heart of it. We are not built to recover from every fall by ourselves.

## Why This Still Matters, Maybe More Than Ever

We live in a culture that often prizes self-sufficiency as a virtue — "I don't need anyone," "I handle my own problems," "I don't want to burden people." Ecclesiastes pushes back against that instinct directly. It doesn't say two are better than one because it's emotionally pleasant; it says it because it's practically, structurally true. You will fall. Something will knock you down — illness, failure, grief, a bad decision, burnout. The question Ecclesiastes forces is: when it happens, is there anyone there to lift you up?

Building real community and friendship takes initiative most of us resist. It means staying in relationships long enough for them to become load-bearing, being honest about struggles instead of performing fine, and showing up for other people's falls so there's mutual trust when yours comes. If you've been isolating yourself — whether from busyness, hurt, or sheer habit — this verse is a quiet nudge to reconnect. Call the friend you've been avoiding. Join the group you keep skipping. Let someone close enough to actually see you fall, because that's also the only way they'll be close enough to help you up.

PRAYER: Lord, I confess I've tried to carry too much of life on my own, believing that was strength instead of recognizing it as isolation. Thank you for designing me for community, not for constant self-reliance. Bring people into my life who will walk closely enough to notice when I stumble, and give me the humility to let them help. Show me who I can be that kind of friend to as well. Teach me that two really are better than one. In Jesus' name, amen.
