## A Line Tucked Inside Strange Instructions

Luke 10 opens with Jesus sending out seventy disciples ahead of him, two by two, into towns he intended to visit. The instructions are strange to modern ears: take no purse, no bag, no extra shoes, and stay in whatever house welcomes you, eating what's set in front of you. Then comes this small, almost offhand line: "the labourer is worthy of his hire."

It's easy to read past this phrase, but it's doing real theological work. Jesus is telling his disciples not to feel guilty or beggarly about receiving support for their labor. Ministry wasn't meant to be charity extracted through pity; it was meant to be the natural, fair exchange for real work being done. The phrase itself was likely a known proverb of the time, and Paul later quotes this exact teaching in 1 Timothy 5:18, applying it to those who labor in preaching and teaching. It had staying power because it named something people instinctively understood: real work deserves real compensation.

## Dignity, Not Just Payment

What's significant here isn't primarily an argument about wages—it's an argument about dignity. The seventy weren't told to feel ashamed for needing food and shelter while doing kingdom work. They were told their labor had value, and that value warranted response. This has something important to say to anyone today who struggles to feel like their work and career matter, especially in jobs that go unnoticed or underappreciated.

Work, in the biblical imagination, was never meant to be separated from dignity. Genesis shows humanity given work before the fall ever happened—tending the garden wasn't a curse, it was purpose. Jesus affirming that laborers deserve their hire fits into that larger pattern: honest work is honorable, and it's appropriate to expect honest return for it, whether that return is a paycheck, respect, or simply the basic provisions of life.

## What This Means for the Career You're In Right Now

If you're someone who feels guilty asking to be paid fairly, or who has internalized the idea that your work and career should somehow be sacrificial to the point of self-neglect, this verse pushes back gently but firmly. There's a difference between generosity and being taken advantage of. Jesus himself, sending workers out on urgent, costly mission, still affirmed that their basic needs mattered and deserved to be met.

This also reframes how we might think about the work we do, paid or unpaid. If labor is worthy of hire, then labor itself is worth taking seriously—not phoning it in, not treating your job as beneath your real calling, but bringing genuine effort because the work itself has dignity. Colossians 3:23 says to work heartily as for the Lord; Luke 10:7 is almost the other side of that coin, affirming that such work deserves to be honored in return.

## A Small Verse With a Large Application

You might not be a traveling missionary dependent on a stranger's hospitality, but the principle translates directly into your career today: don't be ashamed of fair wages, don't undervalue your labor, and don't let guilt talk you into working for less than what's right. At the same time, if you're in a position to pay or compensate others—employees, contractors, the person who mows your lawn—this verse is a quiet but clear instruction to do so fairly and without delay.

Work was never meant to be extracted without honor. Whatever your career looks like this week, you can hold both truths together: work diligently, as Colossians says, and trust that honest labor is, as Jesus said, worthy of its hire.
