## A Letter Written to Settle an Argument

John wrote his first letter late in his life, likely from Ephesus, to churches being pulled apart by false teachers who claimed special spiritual knowledge while showing little actual love for others. Some of them seem to have separated "knowing God" from "loving people"—as if you could have deep theology and cold relationships at the same time. John won't allow that split. Earlier in chapter 4 he writes plainly that anyone who doesn't love doesn't know God, "for God is love." Then he lands on this short, almost offhand sentence: "We love him, because he first loved us."

It's easy to read past a verse this brief, but it quietly dismantles a very human assumption—that love is something we generate and then offer to God as a kind of payment or proof. John reverses the order entirely. Our love isn't the starting point. It's the echo.

## Love as Response, Not Achievement

The structure of the Greek here emphasizes sequence: He loved first; we love as a result. This matters because so much of our spiritual effort is built around trying to love God enough, trying to feel love intensely enough, trying to prove our devotion through performance. But John is saying that any real love in us is downstream of a love that already moved toward us first—while we were, as he says a few verses earlier, still sinners in need of propitiation (1 John 4:10).

This reframes what love even is. It's not a transaction where we love God first to earn His love back. It's not a ladder we climb to become worthy of affection. It's a response to something already given, already finished, already secured in Christ. When you understand this, love stops being exhausting performance and starts being grateful reflection.

## What This Means for Loving Other People

John doesn't stop at our love for God—two verses later he insists that if we love God we must also love our brother. The logic only works because of verse 19. We can love others—even difficult, ungrateful, wounding people—not by digging deep for willpower we don't have, but by drawing from a love that was already poured into us first. Marriages, friendships, and families often strain under the weight of "I'll love you if you love me first." That's not love; that's negotiation. The love John describes moves first, without guarantee of return, because that's exactly how it was shown to us.

If you're running low on love today—toward God, toward a spouse, toward someone who's hurt you—the answer isn't to try harder to manufacture it. It's to go back to the source. Sit with the fact that He loved you first, while you had nothing to offer Him. Let that settle in before you try to extend love outward. Real, lasting love always starts there—not as something you build, but as something you receive and then let overflow.

PRAYER: Father, thank You for loving me first, long before I had any love to offer back. Forgive me for the times I've treated love like something to earn or a transaction to negotiate. Help me to receive Your love so fully that it overflows naturally toward the people around me. Where my love has run dry—in my marriage, my friendships, my own heart—refill me from Your source, not my effort. Teach me to love others the way You first loved me. I'm grateful today for a love I didn't have to earn.
