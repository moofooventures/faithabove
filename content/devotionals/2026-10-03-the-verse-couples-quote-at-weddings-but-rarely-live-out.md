## An Ancient Verse With a Radical Idea

Genesis 2:24 sits right after the creation of Eve, and it reads almost like a narrator's commentary breaking into the story—as if Moses paused to explain why marriage works the way it does. In the ancient Near East, a man typically stayed tied to his father's household for life; family loyalty and inheritance ran through that line. For Genesis to say a man would "leave" his father and mother was genuinely countercultural. It wasn't about geography. It was about shifting primary allegiance.

The Hebrew word for "cleave," dabaq, is intense—it's used elsewhere to describe something stuck fast, glued, or clinging with force. This isn't casual companionship. It's a deliberate, ongoing choice to bond yourself to another person more tightly than to anyone else, including the family that raised you. And then comes the phrase that gives marriage its theological weight: "one flesh." Not just one household, one bed, or one bank account—one new unit that didn't exist before.

## Why Leaving Is Harder Than It Sounds

We often picture "leaving" as a single event—a wedding day, a change of address. But in marriage, leaving is ongoing. It means leaving old patterns of putting your parents' opinions above your spouse's. It means leaving the independence of single life, where every decision only affected you. It means leaving the instinct to run home—emotionally or literally—every time marriage gets hard, instead of running toward your spouse.

Many marriages struggle not because love disappeared, but because leaving never fully happened. One or both partners keep one foot in old loyalties, old comforts, old identities, and never fully cleave to the new one God created. Genesis 2:24 suggests that a strong marriage requires both motions at once: a real leaving and a real cleaving. You can't build "one flesh" while still living like two separate ones who happen to share a house.

## What Cleaving Looks Like on an Ordinary Tuesday

Cleaving isn't mainly about grand romantic gestures. It's built in small, repeated choices: choosing to talk through a disagreement instead of shutting down, choosing your spouse's need for rest over your to-do list, choosing honesty even when a comfortable lie would be easier. It's the daily decision to keep being glued to this one person, especially on the days when distance would feel more convenient.

If you're married, Genesis 2:24 is worth returning to—not as a verse for the wedding program, but as a working description of the relationship you're in right now. Have you truly left what needs leaving? Are you cleaving, actively, today? Marriage was never meant to be two individuals who simply coexist well. It was designed to be one new thing, formed through leaving and held together through cleaving, day after day, year after year.

If you're not married but hoping to be someday, let this verse shape what you're looking for and becoming: someone capable of leaving old loyalties and cleaving fully to one person for life. That kind of love isn't automatic. It's built.
