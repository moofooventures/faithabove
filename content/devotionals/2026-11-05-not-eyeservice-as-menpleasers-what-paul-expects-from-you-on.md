## Written to People Who Couldn't Quit

Paul wrote this instruction to bondservants—people with no legal right to walk away from their jobs, no HR department, and often no hope of advancement. It would have been easy, and understandable, for Paul to simply tell them to endure. Instead he does something more radical: he reframes the entire meaning of their labor. The phrase "eyeservice, as menpleasers" describes work calibrated entirely to whoever is watching—effort that rises when the boss walks by and drops the moment he's gone.

That phrase has aged remarkably well. Eyeservice is still the default posture of most workplaces: we perform for whoever evaluates us, adjusting effort based on who's watching and what's being measured. Paul doesn't condemn the workers for this tendency—he names it because he's offering something different, not more guilt about hustling harder for human approval.

## From the Heart, Not From the Audience

The alternative Paul gives isn't "work harder so you look better." It's a change of audience: "doing the will of God from the heart... as to the Lord, and not to men." This is a complete relocation of who your work is ultimately for. It doesn't erase your actual boss or job description—Paul isn't telling slaves to ignore their masters. He's saying the deepest motivation underneath the task shifts from human approval to something steadier: service rendered to Christ himself, who sees the parts of your work no supervisor or performance review ever will.

This matters enormously for how we experience career and work today. Eyeservice work is exhausting because it's never satisfied—there's always another audience to please, another person whose opinion might shift. Work done "as to the Lord" has a fixed, unchanging audience of one, which paradoxically frees you to do consistent, good-faith work regardless of whether anyone notices. The phrase "with good will" suggests this isn't gritted-teeth obligation; it's work offered willingly, because it means something beyond the paycheck or the praise.

## What This Looks Like in an Ordinary Job

Most of us aren't doing dramatic, visibly "spiritual" work. We're answering emails, finishing reports, show up to shifts that don't feel like ministry. Paul's point is that the mundane nature of the task doesn't disqualify it from being offered to God—in fact, that's exactly the kind of work he's addressing. The grind of unnoticed, unglamorous labor is precisely where eyeservice tends to collapse first, because there's no one around to perform for. That's also precisely where "doing the will of God from the heart" becomes most visible, because it's work maintained purely out of integrity, not incentive.

If your career feels thankless right now, or if you've noticed your own effort rising and falling based on who's supervising you, this passage offers something to actually practice: choose one task today and do it with full effort specifically because no one's watching, as an act of worship rather than compliance. Over time, this reorients not just your output but your experience of work itself—from a performance under constant evaluation to a quiet offering, made with good will, to the one Paul says is always paying attention.
