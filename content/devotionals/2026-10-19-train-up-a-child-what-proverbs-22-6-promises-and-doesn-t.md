## A Proverb, Not a Promise With Fine Print

Few verses carry more weight in a parent's heart — or more guilt — than Proverbs 22:6. It gets quoted at baby dedications and whispered in despair when a grown child walks away from faith. But Proverbs is wisdom literature, not a legal contract. The book itself tells you this in its opening chapter: these are sayings meant to give "subtilty to the simple," general truths about how life tends to work, not ironclad guarantees for every individual case. Reading it as a formula — do this, get that result every time — actually misreads the genre.

The Hebrew phrase translated "train up" is chanak, a word used elsewhere for dedicating a house or initiating something for its intended use. It carries the idea of creating a taste for something, almost like how Hebrew midwives would rub a newborn's gums with chewed dates to stimulate a desire to nurse. Parenting, in this picture, isn't primarily about control or correction — it's about cultivating appetite. You're not just restraining bad behavior; you're forming what a child hungers for.

## "In the Way He Should Go"

Here's a detail many miss: the Hebrew text actually says "according to his way" — there's a personal pronoun built into the phrase that most translations smooth over. That's not teaching every child identically; it's recognizing the bent, the temperament, the particular way a given child is wired, and directing that specific child according to it. Solomon, writing to a nation full of different kinds of kids being raised by different kinds of parents, understood that wise parenting pays attention to the individual child in front of you rather than applying a single mold to every one.

This reframes a lot of parenting anxiety. If your child is wired differently than you expected, or differently than their siblings, Proverbs isn't demanding you force sameness. It's asking you to study this particular child and aim your training at who they actually are.

## Parenting for the Long Middle

The back half of the verse — "when he is old, he will not depart from it" — has comforted and tormented parents in equal measure. It's worth noticing the verse doesn't promise a child will never wander. Old age, not adolescence, is the horizon in view. Many prodigals in Scripture and in life take long roads before coming home — the father in Luke 15 didn't stop being a father during the son's years in the far country, and he didn't stop watching the road either.

So what does faithful parenting look like today, in light of this proverb? It looks like forming appetite more than enforcing compliance — modeling what you actually love, not just what you forbid. It looks like paying attention to this child's particular way, rather than parenting by formula or comparison. And it looks like trusting the long arc rather than panicking over a single season, especially the turbulent middle years when nothing about a wandering teenager looks like training has taken root.

Parenting is costly, uncertain work, and this proverb was never meant to erase that uncertainty — it was meant to steady you inside it. You plant according to the child's way. You trust the growing season to God.
