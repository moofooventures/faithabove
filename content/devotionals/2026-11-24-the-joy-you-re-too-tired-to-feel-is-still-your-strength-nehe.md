## A Crowd Weeping Over a Book

The scene behind this verse is one of the more moving moments in the Old Testament. The exiles have returned to Jerusalem, the walls are finally rebuilt, and Ezra the priest stands on a wooden platform and reads the Law aloud to the whole assembled community -- men, women, and anyone old enough to understand. It's likely the first time in a generation that many of them have heard it clearly explained. And their response is weeping. Hearing how far they and their ancestors had drifted from God's covenant hits them hard, right there in the street.

It's into that raw, grief-filled moment that Nehemiah, Ezra, and the Levites step in with an unexpected command: stop crying, go celebrate. 'This day is holy to the Lord,' they say -- not a day for mourning, but a day to eat rich food, drink something sweet, and share with those who have nothing prepared. And then comes the line that has outlived every other detail of the story: 'the joy of the Lord is your strength.'

## Strength That Doesn't Wait for the Feeling to Come First

It's worth noticing what this verse is not saying. It isn't telling the people to fake happiness or deny what they're grieving. Their tears were legitimate; the text doesn't rebuke them for crying. What it redirects is where they go next. Mourning over past failure wasn't meant to be the final word -- joy rooted in who God is and what he had just done (rebuilding the wall, restoring the Law, re-gathering the people) was meant to carry them forward into strength for what came next.

That distinction matters for anyone running on empty today. Strength, in this verse, isn't gritted-teeth endurance or forcing a smile you don't feel. It's something closer to fuel -- the kind that comes from remembering who God is and what he has already done, even in a season that still holds real grief. The Hebrew word for joy here, simchah, carries a sense of gladness that shows up in celebration, feasting, community -- it's active, not just an internal feeling. The people were told to practically embody joy: eat well, share generously, celebrate together -- and let that lived joy become the thing that steadies them.

## Practicing Joy as a Source of Strength

If you're depleted right now -- physically, emotionally, spiritually -- this verse offers something different from the usual advice to just push harder. It suggests that strength often flows downstream from joy, and joy often needs to be practiced before it's fully felt. That might look like actually pausing to remember a specific thing God has done for you, naming it out loud instead of letting it stay a vague, background gratitude. It might look like sharing what you have with someone who has less, the way Nehemiah told the people to send portions to those with nothing prepared -- generosity that creates joy rather than just waiting to feel joyful enough to be generous.

Strength built this way doesn't collapse the moment circumstances get hard again, because it was never built on circumstances in the first place. It's rooted in the character of a God who rebuilds walls, restores what was lost, and meets his people even in the rubble of their own history. You don't have to manufacture joy from nothing today. You just have to remember who it comes from, and let that remembering become your strength for whatever this day actually requires of you.

PRAYER: Lord, I come to You tired, running on far less than I need. Thank You that my strength doesn't have to come from gritting my teeth, but from the joy that comes from knowing You. Be my strength when mine has run out completely. In Jesus' name, amen.
