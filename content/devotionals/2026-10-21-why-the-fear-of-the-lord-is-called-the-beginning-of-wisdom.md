## Where Wisdom Actually Starts

Proverbs 9 closes out the long opening section of the book with a vivid scene: Wisdom, personified as a woman, has built her house, set her table, and sent out invitations (9:1-6), while Folly does the same with a cheaper, louder imitation down the street (9:13-18). Right in the middle of these competing invitations sits verse 10, almost like a key that unlocks everything else in the book. Before Proverbs hands you a single practical instruction about money, speech, friendship, or work, it insists on a starting point: the fear of the Lord.

The Hebrew word for "beginning" here, reshith, doesn't just mean step one in a sequence you can move past later. It means the foundation, the source, the governing principle everything else is built on. You don't graduate from the fear of the Lord into advanced wisdom that no longer needs it. Every other kind of wisdom in Proverbs — about work, speech, money, relationships — draws its structural integrity from this one root. Pull the root and the rest collapses, however clever it looks on the surface.

## Fear That Isn't About Being Scared

This has tripped up readers for centuries, because "fear" in English usually implies dread. But the fear of the Lord in Hebrew wisdom literature describes something closer to a clear-eyed recognition of who God actually is — infinitely holy, infinitely powerful, infinitely good — paired with a willingness to order your life around that reality instead of your own preferences. It's the opposite of the casual, consumer-style approach many of us bring to faith, where God is a resource to tap for comfort rather than a Being whose holiness reorders everything.

This matters enormously for how we pursue wisdom today. Our culture treats wisdom as pure information — read enough, research enough, optimize enough, and you'll make good decisions. Proverbs disagrees. It says you can accumulate facts for a lifetime and still be a fool if you've never reckoned with God as God. Wisdom, biblically, isn't primarily about IQ. It's about a right-ordered relationship with reality, starting with the Creator of it.

## Knowledge of the Holy: The Verse's Second Half

The parallel line — "the knowledge of the holy is the beginning of understanding" — reinforces the same idea from another angle. "The Holy" here likely refers to God himself (some translations render it "the Holy One"), and "knowledge" in Hebrew wisdom literature, yada, implies intimate, relational knowing rather than mere data collection. It's the same word used for a husband and wife "knowing" each other. Understanding, then, doesn't begin with information about God; it begins with actually knowing him.

For someone wrestling with a hard decision today — a career move, a relationship question, a parenting dilemma — this verse offers a sequence worth following. Before you consult the spreadsheet, the pros-and-cons list, or even well-meaning friends, start by asking whether you're actually walking in reverence toward God in this situation. Are you willing to let his revealed character and commands shape the decision, even if it's not what you'd naturally choose? James 1:5 later promises that God gives wisdom generously to those who ask — but that promise sits inside a relationship already oriented by the fear of the Lord. Wisdom isn't a formula you extract from Scripture and then apply independently of God. It's fruit that grows out of walking closely with him, continually, one decision at a time.

PRAYER: Lord, I want real wisdom, not just more information. Teach me to fear you rightly — not with anxious dread, but with reverent awe that reorders how I live. Forgive me for the times I've tried to solve my problems with cleverness instead of first seeking you. Help me know you more intimately, not just know facts about you. Give me wisdom for the decisions in front of me today, wisdom that starts with you and flows from walking closely beside you. Amen.
