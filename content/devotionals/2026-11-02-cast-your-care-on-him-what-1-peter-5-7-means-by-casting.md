## A Verb That Means More Than 'Let Go'

1 Peter 5:7 is one of the most quoted verses for anxiety, and it's usually shortened to a soft suggestion: give God your worries. But the Greek verb behind 'casting,' *epiripto*, is far more physical than that. It's the same word used elsewhere in the New Testament for throwing a cloak over a donkey's back (Luke 19:35). It means to hurl, to throw with force, to fling something off yourself onto something else entirely. This isn't a gentle handoff. It's the picture of someone finally unloading a weight they were never built to carry.

Peter wrote this letter to Christians scattered across Asia Minor who were facing real pressure—social hostility, economic strain, and the threat of persecution. These weren't people anxious about minor inconveniences; they had legitimate reasons for fear. And it's to that audience, mid-crisis, that Peter gives this instruction. Anxiety wasn't something they were supposed to white-knuckle through with more willpower. It was something to be thrown, deliberately and completely, onto God.

## Why the Casting Works

The second half of the verse is what makes the first half possible: 'for he careth for you.' The Greek word for 'careth' here, *melei*, implies ongoing, active concern—not a distant deity tolerating your problems, but one who is genuinely invested in your wellbeing. This matters because casting your anxiety onto someone who doesn't actually care would just be irresponsible. You'd be setting the weight down on ground that can't hold it. Peter is saying the opposite is true: God's capacity to care, and His willingness to carry what you hand Him, is the very reason the casting makes sense.

This verse comes directly after Peter tells believers to humble themselves under God's mighty hand (verse 6). That ordering isn't accidental. Anxiety often has a quiet pride buried inside it—the sense that we have to hold everything together, that the outcome depends on our ability to manage it. Casting your care on God requires admitting you can't carry it, which is itself an act of humility before it's an act of relief.

## What Casting Looks Like This Week

Anxiety rarely leaves in one dramatic motion. It tends to creep back, especially for the same recurring worries—finances, health, a relationship, a decision you can't undo. Casting your care isn't usually a one-time event; it's a practice you return to, sometimes multiple times a day, every time the weight tries to climb back onto your shoulders.

A practical way to live this out is to name the specific anxiety out loud or on paper, then just as specifically hand it to God in prayer, rather than offering Him a vague 'take my worries' and carrying on exactly as before. Say what it actually is: the bill you can't cover, the test results you're waiting on, the conversation you're dreading. Throw that specific thing, not a general feeling, onto Him. Then, when it returns—and it will—do it again. The casting is allowed to be repetitive, because His care for you doesn't run out between attempts.

If anxiety has convinced you that you're the only one who can hold your life together, 1 Peter 5:7 is a direct challenge to that lie. You were never meant to carry it. He is both strong enough and willing to take it, which is exactly the combination anxious hearts need most.

PRAYER: Father, I confess I've been carrying weight that was never mine to hold alone. Thank You that You don't just tolerate my anxiety but actually care about what's causing it. Help me to specifically name what's weighing on me and throw it onto You, not just once but every time it tries to come back. Teach me the humility it takes to admit I can't manage this by myself. Thank You for being strong enough and willing enough to carry what I bring You. In Jesus' name, amen.
