## A Farming Image for a Tired Heart

Paul wrote Galatians to churches caught up in exhausting arguments about law and grace, and by chapter six he's turned practical, talking about how believers should treat each other even when it's draining. The phrase "well doing" doesn't mean one heroic act -- it's the ongoing, repetitive work of showing up for people, doing the right thing, staying faithful in small tasks nobody applauds. The Greek word behind "be weary" (enkakeo) carries the idea of losing heart to the point of giving up entirely, not just feeling a little tired. Paul knew his readers weren't lazy. They were worn out.

Then he reaches for agricultural language his audience would have understood instantly: sowing and reaping. Farmers in first-century Galatia didn't plant in the morning and harvest by afternoon. There were months of waiting, weather they couldn't control, and seasons where nothing visible happened at all even though growth was taking place underground. "In due season" is Paul's way of saying the harvest is coming, but it operates on God's timetable, not ours. Perseverance, in this verse, isn't gritting your teeth through one hard day -- it's staying planted through an entire season you can't rush.

## Why Perseverance Feels So Lonely

One of the hardest parts of perseverance is that it rarely looks impressive from the outside. The parent who keeps showing up for a struggling child, the employee who keeps doing honest work in a thankless job, the person praying for a breakthrough that hasn't come -- none of that makes headlines. It can feel like you're the only one still trying while everyone else has moved on. But Paul's warning isn't aimed at people who've already quit; it's aimed at people who are still in the field, tempted to walk away right before the harvest.

There's a reason this verse has comforted so many people over centuries of hard seasons: it doesn't promise the work will stay easy, it promises the work isn't wasted. The exhaustion you feel doing good -- loving a difficult person, staying honest when cutting corners would be simpler, showing up to pray again when the answer hasn't come -- is real. Paul doesn't minimize it. He just insists that fainting now would cost you the very thing you've been working toward.

## Staying Planted Today

If you're in a season where perseverance feels like the only thing holding you together, start by naming exactly what "well doing" looks like for you right now. Maybe it's a relationship you haven't given up on, a habit of prayer you're maintaining through silence, or simply getting up and doing the next right thing when you'd rather not. Paul's promise doesn't require you to feel strong. It requires you to stay in the field.

Practically, that might mean lowering the bar for what counts as faithfulness today -- not performing, just continuing. It might mean telling someone you trust that you're close to fainting, so you're not carrying the weariness alone. And it might mean remembering that "due season" is a season you don't control, which means the waiting itself is part of the obedience, not a sign you're doing something wrong. The farmer who stops watering in July because the corn isn't tall yet never sees August. Don't let today be the day you stop.
