## Written From a Place of Need

Psalm 28 doesn't open with confidence—it opens with David crying out, afraid God will be silent to him, afraid he'll end up like those who go down to the pit. This is not a psalm of a man who feels strong. It's a psalm of a man who has run out of strength and is asking God not to leave him there. By verse 7, something has shifted—not because David's circumstances changed, but because he remembered where his strength actually comes from.

The Hebrew word for 'strength' here, oz, carries the sense of might, force, a fortified place. David isn't saying God gives him strength the way a vitamin gives you energy. He's saying God himself is the strength—the thing he stands on when his own legs give out. Paired with 'shield' (magen), the image is of a warrior who has nothing left to defend himself with, and finds that the LORD has become both the sword arm and the cover.

## Strength That Doesn't Start With Trying Harder

We tend to think of strength as something we build—willpower, discipline, grit. And there's a place for those things. But this psalm locates strength somewhere else entirely: 'my heart trusted in him, and I am helped.' The order matters. Trust came first. The help—the strength—followed. David didn't white-knuckle his way into feeling strong; he trusted, and in the trusting, he was carried.

This is strange good news for anyone exhausted by trying to manufacture their own resilience. If you've been gritting your teeth through a hard season, trying to will yourself into strength you don't have, this verse offers a different path. The strength isn't found by trying harder to be strong. It's found by trusting the One who already is strong, and letting that trust become the ground you stand on. This is why Scripture elsewhere says those who wait on the Lord renew their strength—not those who exhaust themselves trying to produce it alone.

## The Strange Order: Rejoicing Before Resolution

Notice too that David's heart 'greatly rejoiceth' and he sings praise—not after his enemies are defeated, not after the psalm resolves into victory, but in the same breath as his trust. The joy isn't a reaction to an answered prayer yet; it's a response to who God already is. This is strength expressing itself not as stoic endurance but as song. Real strength in the biblical sense isn't grim; it has music in it.

If you need strength today—for a diagnosis, a financial strain, a relationship that's falling apart, a grief that won't lift—this psalm doesn't tell you to pretend you're fine. It shows you a man who was desperate, who cried out honestly, and who found that trusting God was itself the turning point. Strength, in this sense, isn't the absence of need. It's the presence of God met by trust.

Try praying this psalm today, maybe out loud. Say 'the LORD is my strength' even if you don't feel it yet. David wrote this before the outcome was settled, trusting that the God who had helped him before would help him again. Let your heart trust first. The strength—and even the song—tends to follow.

PRAYER: Lord, I come to You worn out and low on strength of my own. Thank You for being my strength and my shield when I have nothing left to offer. I choose to trust You even before I see the outcome change. Help my heart to rejoice in who You are, not just in what You do for me. Carry me today, Lord, the way You carried David. Amen.
