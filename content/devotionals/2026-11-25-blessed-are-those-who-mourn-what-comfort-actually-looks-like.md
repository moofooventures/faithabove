## A Strange Word to Call Grief

Matthew 5:4 is one of the hardest Beatitudes to sit with, because it doesn't tell grieving people to stop grieving. Jesus says "blessed are they that mourn," using the Greek word *penthountes*, which describes the deepest, most visible kind of mourning — the sort associated with sackcloth, ashes, and wailing in the Old Testament. This isn't a quiet, composed sadness. It's grief and loss that the whole community would have seen and recognized. And Jesus doesn't call it weakness or a lack of faith. He calls it blessed.

That word would have startled His first listeners. In a culture that often equated suffering with divine punishment, Jesus flips the script entirely. He's sitting on a hillside, likely surrounded by people who had buried children, lost land to Rome, or watched loved ones die too soon — and He tells them their mourning is not evidence that God has abandoned them. It's actually a place where God draws near.

## Comfort That Comes, Not Comfort That's Forced

The promise attached to this mourning is comfort — *paraklesis* in Greek, the same root word used for the Holy Spirit as Comforter in John's Gospel. It's not a vague feeling of being okay eventually. It's the idea of someone coming alongside you, sitting in the loss with you, not rushing you out of it. Grief and loss in Scripture are never treated as problems to be solved quickly. Job's friends sat with him in silence for seven days before anyone spoke. The Psalms are full of raw laments that never apologize for their sorrow. Jesus Himself wept at Lazarus's tomb, even knowing He was about to raise him.

What this tells us is that mourning isn't a detour from faith — it can be an expression of it. To grieve deeply is to acknowledge that loss matters, that the person or the dream or the life you lost was real and valuable. Scripture never asks you to pretend otherwise.

## Living With Grief Today

If you're carrying grief and loss right now, this verse isn't asking you to hurry toward comfort you don't feel yet. It's simply promising that comfort is coming, and that your mourning hasn't disqualified you from blessing — it's actually named as one of the places blessing shows up. Practically, this might mean giving yourself permission to grieve without a timeline. It might mean inviting people into your sorrow instead of performing strength you don't have. It might mean praying honest, even angry, prayers, the way the psalmists did.

The comfort Jesus promises often arrives through ordinary things: a friend who shows up without trying to fix anything, a memory that brings a small laugh instead of only pain, a quiet sense that you are not alone in the dark. These are not consolation prizes. They are the beginning of the comfort this verse promises, and they are proof that God meets grief and loss not with distance, but with presence.
