## A Letter to People Who Had No Career Ladder

Paul wrote Colossians while under house arrest in Rome, but the verse itself is aimed at an audience we might not expect: slaves. In the ancient Roman household, bondservants made up a huge portion of the workforce, and their labor was rarely their own choice, their hours were not negotiable, and promotion was not on the table. Into that reality Paul doesn't say "endure it" or "wait for freedom." He says work heartily, as to the Lord, not to men. The Greek word behind "heartily" is *ek psyches* -- literally, "from the soul." Paul is asking for work that comes from the deepest part of a person, aimed at God, regardless of who is technically signing the paycheck or giving the orders.

That context matters for how we hear this verse today. Most of us have more freedom than a first-century servant, yet we still complain about commutes, micromanaging bosses, and jobs that don't feel like "calling." Paul's framework cuts underneath all of that. He's not grading the job description. He's addressing the posture of the worker.

## When Work Feels Like It Doesn't Matter

There's a particular kind of tiredness that comes from feeling like your work is invisible -- the spreadsheet no one reads, the meal no one thanks you for, the project reassigned at the last minute. Colossians 3:23 doesn't promise your boss will notice. It promises Someone already has. The very next verse says you will "receive the reward of the inheritance" from the Lord, not from HR. That's a complete relocation of the audience you're performing for.

This doesn't mean work stops being hard or unjust situations stop needing to change. Paul himself elsewhere tells masters to treat servants justly (Colossians 4:1). But it does mean your sense of worth and effort doesn't have to rise and fall with whether a manager praises you this quarter. Your work and career become an act of worship the moment you remember who you're actually clocking in for.

## Practical Shape for Monday Morning

What does "heartily, as to the Lord" actually look like at 8 a.m. on a Tuesday? It looks like finishing the email thread with the same care you'd want if Christ were reading it over your shoulder -- because, in a sense, He is. It looks like not cutting corners when no one's watching, because the audience was never only the people watching. It looks like resisting the temptation to see your job as meaningless filler between weekends, and instead asking what faithfulness looks like inside this specific desk, this specific shift, this specific season of your career.

For some readers, this is a season of searching -- job applications, interviews, the particular anxiety of unemployment. The same verse still applies to the job of searching itself: pursue it wholeheartedly, as unto the Lord, trusting Him with the outcome you can't control. For others, the struggle isn't finding work but finding meaning in work you already have. Colossians 3:23 doesn't ask you to manufacture excitement about a job you dislike. It asks you to locate your motivation somewhere sturdier than enthusiasm -- in the character of the One you're ultimately serving.

Work, in this light, stops being a necessary evil squeezed between the parts of life that actually matter, and becomes one of the ordinary places God is forming you. That's a quiet kind of dignity available to the executive and the entry-level hire alike.

PRAYER: Lord, thank You for the work You've given me to do, even on the days it feels thankless. Help me work heartily, as unto You, and not merely to please the people watching. Where I am tired or discouraged in my career, renew my sense of purpose and remind me who I'm really serving. Give me integrity when no one notices and patience when progress is slow. I trust You with the results I cannot control. In Jesus' name, amen.
