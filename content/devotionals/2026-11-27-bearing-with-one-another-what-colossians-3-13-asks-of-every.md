## A Word That Means More Than Tolerating Someone

Paul wrote Colossians from a prison cell, to a church he had never visited, in a city he may never have seen. And yet in the middle of a letter about the supremacy of Christ, he stops to talk about something painfully ordinary: how to live with people who annoy, disappoint, and wound you. The Greek word translated "forbearing" is anechomai -- it means to hold up under, to put up with, to endure something with patience rather than walking away from it. It's the same posture a beam takes when it bears weight. Paul isn't asking the Colossians to pretend offenses don't happen. He's asking them to carry the weight of each other's flaws without collapsing the relationship.

That's a strange thing to ask of people who claim to love each other, until you remember who Paul is writing to. The early church in the Lycus Valley was a patchwork of Jews, Gentiles, slaves, and slave owners all sitting in the same room, all claiming the same Christ. Quarrels weren't hypothetical. Paul names them directly: "if any man have a quarrel against any." He doesn't say if -- he assumes it. Relationships, even godly ones, even covenant ones like marriage, produce friction. The question was never whether people would wrong each other. It was what they'd do next.

## Forgiveness Isn't the Reward for a Good Relationship -- It's the Engine

Here's what trips people up about marriage and close relationships: we tend to think forgiveness is for the big betrayals, the dramatic ruptures. But Colossians 3:13 is written for the daily grind -- the sharp word at dinner, the broken promise, the way someone's tone can still sting years into a marriage. Paul's measuring stick is uncomfortable: "even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye." Not forgive when it's deserved. Not forgive once and keep score after that. Forgive the way you were forgiven -- fully, repeatedly, without a running tab.

This is where most relationship advice stops short. The world will tell you to set boundaries (sometimes wise), to walk away from what doesn't serve you (sometimes necessary), but rarely does it tell you to bear with someone on purpose, as a spiritual discipline. Paul isn't describing a doormat. He's describing a decision -- made daily, sometimes hourly -- to not let an offense become the whole story of the relationship.

## What This Looks Like on a Tuesday

Most marriages aren't broken by one catastrophic event. They erode under the accumulated weight of small, unforgiven things -- the comment that was never addressed, the apology that was never really given, the silent resentment that gets stored instead of released. Forbearance is the daily act of setting that weight down instead of adding it to the pile. It means saying, "I'm choosing not to bring this up again," and meaning it.

If you're in a hard season with a spouse, a friend, a sibling, start smaller than you think you need to. Don't try to forgive the whole history today. Forgive today's offense today. Bear with this one moment. Christ's forgiveness of you wasn't a single transaction either -- it's ongoing, patient, and specific to you, right now, in your actual failures. Relationships that last are rarely the ones without conflict. They're the ones where both people keep choosing, again and again, to forbear.
