## A Legal Argument About Belonging

Paul wrote Galatians to churches being pressured to add law-keeping to their faith—circumcision, dietary rules, ritual observances—as if grace alone weren't enough to secure their place with God. In chapter 4, he builds a legal argument using Roman adoption law, which his readers would have understood instantly. Under Roman custom, an adopted son lost every legal tie to his old family and gained full, irrevocable rights in his new one—often with more security than a biological heir, since an adoption couldn't be undone by a father's later change of heart.

Into that cultural backdrop, Paul drops verse 7 like a thesis statement: you are no longer a servant, but a son. This wasn't abstract theology to his readers. It was a direct challenge to anyone still living as though their relationship with God depended on ongoing performance, like a servant who has to keep proving usefulness to stay employed.

## Servant Thinking vs. Son Thinking

A servant in the ancient world, even a trusted one, lived with constant underlying anxiety: will I still have a place here tomorrow? Status had to be earned daily. A son, by contrast, didn't wake up wondering if he still belonged to the family. His identity wasn't in question, even on his worst day.

This distinction cuts straight to how many believers quietly operate. Much of our spiritual exhaustion comes from functioning like servants trying to earn standing we've already been given. We read our Bibles out of fear rather than relationship. We serve in the church to prove something rather than out of identity in Christ that's already secure. Paul's point is that this entire framework is obsolete. The legal status has changed. You're not auditioning for sonship; you already have it.

## Heir of God: The Second Half of the Verse

Paul doesn't stop at "son"—he adds "heir of God." In Roman law, an heir received the father's full estate, not a portion doled out based on merit. Paul later writes in Romans 8:17 that believers are "joint-heirs with Christ," meaning the inheritance isn't diminished because others share it. Your identity in Christ isn't a watered-down version of Jesus' relationship with the Father—it's the same relationship, extended to you by adoption.

This has enormous weight for anyone who has built their sense of worth on performance, approval, or comparison. Your identity in Christ doesn't fluctuate with your productivity, your mistakes, or how your week has gone. The adoption papers, so to speak, are already signed. You can't un-son yourself through failure, and you didn't son yourself through achievement in the first place.

## Living Like the Inheritance Is Real

Practically, this means catching yourself when you default to servant thinking—when you treat prayer like clocking in, or treat a bad day as proof God is disappointed in you. It means remembering that your identity in Christ was settled before you did anything to deserve or disqualify it. You don't need to perform your way back into the family. You're already in it, already an heir, already secure. Let that truth, not your performance, define how you walk into today.

PRAYER: Father, thank You for making me Your child, not leaving me as a servant trying to earn my place. Forgive me for the days I live like my standing with You depends on how well I perform. Let my identity in Christ shape how I see myself today, not my failures or my achievements. I receive this truth as my own.
