## Not a Wedding Verse Originally

1 Corinthians 13 shows up at nearly every wedding, but Paul didn't write it for brides and grooms. He wrote it to a church in Corinth that was fractured by lawsuits, rivalries, and people flaunting spiritual gifts to outdo each other. Verse 5 specifically addresses behavior: love "seeketh not her own" and "is not easily provoked." Paul is correcting people who were keeping score, defending their own interests, and quick to take offense — the exact dynamics that quietly erode a marriage over time.

The phrase "thinketh no evil" is especially striking in the Greek. The word used, *logizetai*, is an accounting term — it means to keep a ledger, to tally up wrongs. Paul is saying love doesn't keep a record of offenses to bring out later. That's a remarkably practical image for marriage and relationships: how many arguments are really just old entries being pulled back out of a mental ledger?

## What Keeping Score Does to a Marriage

Most couples don't drift apart over one catastrophic event. It's usually smaller things accumulating — an unspoken resentment here, a sarcastic comment there, each one logged and remembered. Paul's word picture names this exact pattern and calls it out as incompatible with real love. "Seeketh not her own" doesn't mean having no needs or opinions; it means love isn't primarily focused on winning, being right, or getting its way.

"Not easily provoked" is also worth pausing on. The Greek word suggests a sharp, sudden irritation — the kind of reaction that comes before you've even thought about it. Paul isn't saying a mature marriage never experiences frustration. He's describing a love that isn't quick to escalate, that gives the benefit of the doubt before assuming the worst.

## Practicing This Kind of Love

For marriage and relationships today, this verse offers something more useful than sentiment — it offers a diagnostic. When tension rises, it's worth asking: am I keeping a ledger right now? Am I more interested in being right than in being close? These aren't rhetorical questions meant to produce guilt; they're an invitation to notice patterns before they calcify into distance.

Practicing 1 Corinthians 13:5 in a real marriage might look like naming a grievance honestly instead of filing it away, choosing to let a minor irritation pass instead of reacting to it, or asking your spouse what they need instead of assuming you already know. It means releasing old scorekeeping — the comment from three years ago, the mistake that's been brought up five times — because love "thinketh no evil" in the sense that it refuses to keep tallying.

This isn't a call to suppress real hurt or avoid necessary conversations. It's a call to stop treating your spouse as an opponent to outmaneuver. Paul wrote this to a divided church because unity required people to stop seeking their own interest first. The same is true in marriage: the relationships that last aren't the ones with no conflict, but the ones where both people have learned to put down the ledger.
