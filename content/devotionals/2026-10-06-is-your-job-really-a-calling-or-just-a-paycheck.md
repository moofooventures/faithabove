## A King Who Tried Everything

Solomon wrote Ecclesiastes after pursuing wealth, achievement, pleasure, and massive building projects — and finding all of it ultimately "vanity," a Hebrew word (*hevel*) that literally means vapor or breath, something that can't be grasped. By the time he writes chapter 2, he's tried working obsessively for legacy and meaning, and it left him empty. This verse comes right after that disillusionment, almost like an exhale: maybe the answer was simpler than he thought all along.

What's striking is that Solomon doesn't land on "find your dream job" or "discover your purpose and everything will feel meaningful." He lands on something much more modest — eat, drink, and let your soul enjoy good in your labor. That's not resignation; in context, it's relief. After exhausting every grand pursuit, he concludes that ordinary enjoyment of ordinary work is itself a gift from God's hand, not a consolation prize.

## Work Without the Weight of Ultimate Meaning

Our culture often puts enormous pressure on work and career to be the source of identity and meaning — find your passion, do what you love, make your job your purpose. Solomon, who had more career success than almost anyone in history, says that chasing meaning that way leads to "vanity and vexation of spirit." The relief in Ecclesiastes 2:24 is that you don't have to extract ultimate significance from your job for the work to be worthwhile. You can simply do it, find real enjoyment where it exists, and receive that enjoyment as something God Himself placed in your hands.

This doesn't mean work is meaningless — Ecclesiastes elsewhere affirms work has real value and dignity. It means the weight of meaning doesn't rest on the career itself. It rests on receiving each day's labor, and its simple pleasures, as a gift rather than a performance.

## Monday Morning Application

If your job feels unglamorous — spreadsheets, shift work, meetings that could have been emails — this verse gives permission to stop measuring it against some imagined "calling" you think you're missing. It invites you to notice smaller things: satisfaction in a task done well, a good conversation with a coworker, the simple rhythm of earning a living and coming home to eat and rest. Those aren't small consolations. Solomon calls them "from the hand of God."

And if your career does feel meaningful and purposeful right now, this verse is still worth sitting with, because it guards against turning that career into an idol — something you're relying on to answer questions only God can answer about your worth and purpose.

Either way, the practical move is the same: do the work in front of you, let yourself actually enjoy what's enjoyable about it instead of always looking past it to the next thing, and receive that enjoyment as a gift rather than something you had to earn through bigger achievement. Solomon tried bigger. This is what he came back to.
