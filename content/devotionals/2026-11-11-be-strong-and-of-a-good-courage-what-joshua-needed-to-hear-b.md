## A Command, Not a Suggestion

Moses had just died. The man who had led Israel for forty years, who had parted seas and spoken with God face to face, was gone, and Joshua was left holding the responsibility of leading over a million people into a land full of walled cities and hostile armies. It's easy to read 'be strong and of a good courage' as a nice motivational line. It wasn't. It was a command, repeated three times in this chapter alone (verses 6, 7, 9), because God knew exactly how afraid Joshua actually was.

The Hebrew chazaq ('be strong') and ematz ('of a good courage') are both imperatives—not wishes, not blessings, but direct orders. God doesn't ask Joshua how he feels about the assignment. He commands courage the way a general commands a soldier to hold the line, because the mission depends on it and because feelings, left unchallenged, would have sent Joshua running the other direction.

## Courage Isn't the Absence of Fear

Notice the phrasing: 'be not afraid, neither be thou dismayed.' God doesn't say Joshua has no reason to be afraid—crossing the Jordan into Canaan was genuinely dangerous. Rather, God is telling Joshua not to let fear have the final word, not to let it dictate his actions. Biblical courage was never the absence of fear; it's obedience in spite of it. Joshua almost certainly felt the dread of the task ahead. The command was to act anyway, strengthened by something outside his own nerve.

That 'something' is the final clause, the actual engine of the whole verse: 'for the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest.' The courage isn't self-generated. It's not Joshua pumping himself up. It's grounded entirely in God's presence going with him into every place he would go—every battle, every negotiation, every sleepless night before Jericho. Take away that clause and the command would be cruel. With it, the command becomes possible.

## What This Means for Your Own Jordan River

Most of us aren't leading armies into Canaan, but we know what it is to stand at the edge of something that requires courage—a diagnosis, a move, a new job, a conversation we've been avoiding, a relationship we need to end or begin. The fear that rises in those moments is not a sign of faithlessness; it's a sign you understand the stakes. The question isn't whether you feel brave. It's whether you'll take the step anyway, trusting that the same God who went before Joshua goes before you.

It's worth noticing who was speaking this command. Not a general with a plan, but God himself, who had already parted the sea and would soon stop the Jordan's waters. The courage God commands He also supplies the ground for. He doesn't send Joshua into Canaan alone, and He doesn't send you into your hard thing alone either.

If you're standing at your own river's edge today, afraid of what's ahead, let this verse do what it did for Joshua: not talk you out of your fear, but give you a reason bigger than the fear. Be strong. Be of good courage. Not because the task is small, but because the God who goes with you is not.
