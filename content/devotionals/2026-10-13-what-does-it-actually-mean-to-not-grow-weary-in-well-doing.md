## Farming Language for a Tired Church

Paul wrote this to churches in Galatia that were exhausted—exhausted from doctrinal conflict, exhausted from false teachers twisting the gospel, and likely exhausted from the ordinary grind of doing good without seeing quick results. He reaches for agricultural imagery that every reader in that culture would understand instantly: sowing and reaping. Farmers in first-century Galatia didn't plant seed in the morning and harvest by evening. They planted, then waited through weeks of weather, pests, and uncertainty before anything visible happened. Perseverance wasn't optional for them; it was the only way farming worked.

The phrase 'well doing' in the Greek (kalopoiountes) refers to consistent, ongoing good action—not a single generous act, but a sustained lifestyle of doing right even when no one applauds it. Paul isn't talking about heroic, one-time sacrifice. He's talking about the slow, unglamorous perseverance of continuing to love, give, serve, and obey when you can't yet see the fruit of it.

## Why We Grow Weary

The word translated 'weary' (ekkakomen) carries the idea of losing heart, becoming discouraged to the point of giving up entirely. It's what happens when you've prayed for the same person for years with no visible change, or kept showing up to serve in a ministry that seems to make no difference, or stayed faithful in a hard marriage without seeing the breakthrough you hoped for. Weariness isn't weakness—it's a natural response to investing without immediate return. Paul doesn't shame his readers for feeling tired. He simply reminds them that the season of sowing is not the same as the season of reaping, and giving up during the sowing season guarantees there will be no harvest at all.

This is where perseverance becomes less about willpower and more about perspective. If you believe the harvest is coming, you can endure the waiting. If you've stopped believing, weariness turns into quitting. Paul's whole argument hinges on verse 9's conditional clause: 'if we faint not.' The harvest is promised—but it's promised to those who don't quit in the meantime.

## Practicing Perseverance in the In-Between

So what does 'not growing weary' look like practically? It looks like showing up to pray for your prodigal child again tonight, even though you've prayed the same prayer for years. It looks like staying patient with a difficult coworker, choosing kindness again tomorrow even though yesterday's kindness seemed to go nowhere. It looks like continuing therapy, continuing recovery, continuing the slow work of healing even when progress feels invisible.

Paul's promise is specific: 'in due season we shall reap.' Not immediately. Not on your timeline. But in due season—God's appointed time, which is often later than we'd like but never too late. Perseverance is simply trusting that due season while living faithfully in the meantime.

If you're worn out today from doing good that doesn't seem to be working, this verse isn't a guilt trip—it's a lifeline. The harvest hasn't failed. It's just not here yet. Keep sowing.
