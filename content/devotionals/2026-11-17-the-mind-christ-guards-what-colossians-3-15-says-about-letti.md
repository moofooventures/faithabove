## An Umpire for the Heart

Paul's word choice in Colossians 3:15 is easy to skim past in English, but the original Greek carries a surprisingly vivid picture. "Let the peace of God rule" uses the word brabeuo, which comes directly from the world of athletic contests. A brabeus was the umpire or judge at Greek games—the person whose decision settled disputes and determined who won. Paul is telling the believers in Colossae to let peace act as the umpire of their hearts, the deciding voice whenever anxious thoughts, fears, and competing emotions are arguing for control.

This matters enormously for anyone wrestling with anxiety, because anxiety often feels like several voices shouting different verdicts at once: worst-case scenarios, old fears, what-ifs, pressure to fix everything immediately. Paul isn't suggesting you'll never feel the pull of those voices. He's saying something stronger needs to be given the authority to make the final call. Peace doesn't mean the absence of conflicting feelings; it means something has been appointed judge over them.

## Written to a Divided Church

Colossians 3 was written into a real, specific tension. The church at Colossae was a mixed community—Jews and Gentiles, people with very different backgrounds and assumptions, trying to function as "one body," the phrase Paul uses right in this verse. Anxiety in that church wasn't abstract; it came from real relational friction, cultural misunderstanding, and the pressure of holding a fragile new community together. When Paul says to let peace rule "to the which also ye are called in one body," he's connecting personal anxiety directly to communal life. Your anxious heart doesn't exist in a vacuum—it affects how you treat the people around you, and their posture affects you too.

This is a helpful corrective for anyone who treats anxiety purely as a private, internal battle to manage alone. Paul assumes peace is something cultivated in community, through mutual forgiveness (verse 13), through love that binds everything together (verse 14), and through gratitude, which is exactly where the verse lands: "and be ye thankful." Anxiety and gratitude have a hard time occupying the same space for long. One of the most practical doors out of a spiraling mind is simply naming, out loud, what you're thankful for right now.

## Letting Peace Make the Call

So what does it look like to actually let peace rule instead of anxiety? It starts by noticing when a thought shows up demanding an immediate verdict—a fear about tomorrow, a worry about what someone meant by what they said, a dread about something unresolved. Instead of letting that thought automatically win, picture peace standing as the umpire, asking: does this get the final say, or does it answer to something greater? The peace Paul describes isn't manufactured positivity. It's the peace of God, something given, not something worked up through willpower.

Practically, this might mean pausing before reacting to an anxious thought and asking what's actually true, what's within your control, and what you can hand over to God today. It might mean choosing gratitude on purpose, even in a small way, as a way of letting a different voice into the courtroom of your mind. Anxiety will keep making its case. But Paul's instruction is clear: you get to decide who holds the gavel. Let peace rule, and let thankfulness be the evidence it's working.
