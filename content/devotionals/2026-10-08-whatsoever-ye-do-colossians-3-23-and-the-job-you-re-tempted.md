## Written to People Who Had Every Reason to Coast

Paul wrote this instruction as part of a longer passage addressed specifically to slaves within the Colossian church -- people with no career ladder to climb, no performance review that could change their circumstances, no realistic hope of promotion or raise. If anyone in history had an excuse to do the bare minimum at work, it was them. And yet Paul tells them to work "heartily," a word that in the Greek (ek psyches) literally means "from the soul." Not grudging compliance. Not resentful minimum effort. Whole-souled engagement, even in an unjust system, even without recognition.

That context should reshape how we read this verse for our own work and career. Whatever frustrations you carry about your job -- an unappreciative boss, a dead-end title, a paycheck that doesn't match the effort -- Paul's original audience had it measurably harder, and he still called them to heart-level work. This isn't a verse for people with dream jobs. It's a verse for people doing hard, unglamorous, under-recognized labor, which describes most of us most days.

## Work as Worship, Not Just a Means to an End

The phrase "as to the Lord, and not unto men" relocates the audience of your work and career. Most days, we work "unto men" -- for a manager's approval, a client's satisfaction, a performance score. That's not wrong exactly, but it's an unstable foundation, because people are inconsistent judges. Some days they notice your effort; most days they don't. If your sense of purpose at work depends entirely on human recognition, you will burn out or grow bitter.

Paul offers a different foundation: work done "as to the Lord" has an audience of one who always sees, who never overlooks diligence, and who isn't grading you on comparison with coworkers. This doesn't mean your work and career stop mattering to other people -- it means your motivation and sense of worth get anchored somewhere sturdier than a boss's mood or an inbox full of unanswered emails.

## What This Looks Like on an Ordinary Tuesday

This verse isn't calling you to be a workaholic or to tolerate genuine injustice without seeking change -- Paul addresses masters' responsibilities in the very next chapter, holding employers accountable too. It's calling you to integrity of effort regardless of whether anyone's watching. That might look like finishing a report carefully even though your manager won't read every line. It might mean treating a difficult customer with patience because Christ sees that interaction, even if no survey captures it. It might mean resisting the pull to disengage mentally from a job that no longer excites you, because "heartily" isn't contingent on how meaningful the task feels.

For those wrestling with whether their work and career even matters in God's eyes -- whether only "ministry" jobs count as sacred -- this verse answers plainly: whatsoever ye do. Not just preaching, not just missions work. Spreadsheets, shift work, dishes, code, diapers, deliveries. All of it can be done "as to the Lord."

## A Small Shift for Today

Before you start your next task today, try silently reframing it: "I'm doing this as to the Lord." It won't change your job description, but it can change your posture inside it -- from resentment or autopilot to quiet, soul-level engagement. Work and career, viewed this way, become less about climbing and more about offering, one ordinary task at a time, to the one who sees every bit of it.

PRAYER: Lord, so much of my work feels unseen and unrewarded, and some days I just want to coast through it. Thank You for reminding me that You see every task I do, even the small and thankless ones. Help me to work heartily today, not for the approval of people, but as an offering to You. Give me integrity in my effort and peace about the things I cannot control at my job. Let my work, whatever it is, be done for Your glory. Amen.
