## The Decision That Isn't Really About the Decision

Most of us read Proverbs 3:5-6 while standing at a fork in the road -- which job, which house, which relationship, which city. We want it to function like a GPS: trust God, and the next turn will light up. But the verse is doing something subtler. It's not a formula for extracting the right answer; it's an instruction about where to anchor your confidence while you're still figuring things out. Solomon, writing to his son in the tradition of wisdom literature, uses the Hebrew word batach for "trust" -- it means to lean your full weight on something, the way you'd lean against a wall you were certain wouldn't give way. The command isn't "trust God's plan," which is abstract. It's "trust God," a person, with the same weight you'd put on a wall holding up your house.

## Your Own Understanding Isn't the Villain

"Lean not unto thine own understanding" sounds like Scripture is telling you to switch off your brain, but that's not quite it. Solomon, remember, is the same man who wrote an entire book instructing his son to pursue wisdom and understanding. The issue isn't having understanding; it's leaning on it exclusively, as if your own reasoning -- limited, biased, seeing only today -- is sufficient to map out a future you can't see. Ancient Israelites didn't have the luxury of five-year plans; drought, war, and famine could upend everything overnight. Trusting God wasn't passive resignation -- it was the only rational posture available to people who knew how little control they actually had. We're not so different. We just have more data to fool us into thinking we're in control.

## Acknowledging Him in All Your Ways

The phrase "in all thy ways acknowledge him" is the hinge of the whole passage, and it's easy to rush past. The Hebrew word for "acknowledge" (yada) means to know intimately, not just to tip your hat toward God before making your own call. This is guidance built on relationship, not a vending machine where you insert a prayer and out comes clarity. It means inviting God into the mundane decisions -- the budget, the schedule, the hard conversation -- not just the five big forks in the road. People who practice this kind of ongoing acknowledgment tend to find that when the big decision comes, they're not panicking for direction; they've been walking with Him all along, and the next step feels less like a leap in the dark.

The promise -- "he shall direct thy paths" -- uses a word (yashar) that means to make straight or smooth, like leveling rough terrain. It doesn't promise you'll see the whole road. It promises that the ground right in front of you will be made walkable. If you're stuck right now trying to force clarity out of a decision that won't resolve, maybe the better prayer isn't "show me the whole path" but "help me trust You with the step I can see." Guidance, in Scripture, is rarely a floodlight. It's usually a lamp -- just enough for the next footfall.

PRAYER: Father, I don't know which way to go, and I'm tired of trying to force an answer out of my own reasoning. Help me trust You with my whole heart instead of leaning only on what I can figure out myself. I want to know You, not just use You for direction -- so meet me in the small decisions, not just the big ones. Make my path straight even when I can't see very far ahead. I'm leaning my whole weight on You today. Amen.
