## A Psalm Attributed to the Busiest King in Scripture

Psalm 127 carries the heading "A Song of degrees for Solomon," and the irony is almost too perfect. Here is a man who built the Temple, a palace complex, fortified cities, a navy, and an empire of trade and wealth — the most accomplished builder-king in Israel's history — opening a song by saying that none of it matters unless the Lord builds it. If anyone had the resume to boast about effort and achievement, it was Solomon. Instead, he hands the credit somewhere else entirely.

The imagery is deliberately everyday: builders laying stone on stone, a watchman standing guard through the night shift, someone rising before dawn and staying up after dark just to make ends meet. These are not abstract metaphors; they're the actual rhythms of ancient labor and career — construction, security, long workdays bracketed by darkness on both ends. Sound familiar? Early alarms, late nights, the quiet exhaustion of "the bread of sorrows," as the psalm calls it. Solomon is describing burnout three thousand years before we had a word for it.

## The Problem Isn't Work — It's Working in Vain

It's worth being precise about what this psalm does and doesn't say. It's not condemning work, ambition, or diligence — Scripture elsewhere praises hard work repeatedly (Proverbs is full of warnings against laziness). What Psalm 127 condemns is labor *in vain* — effort disconnected from dependence on God, work driven by anxious self-reliance rather than partnership with him. The watchman still needs to watch. The builder still needs to build. But if either believes the outcome rests entirely on their own vigilance and sweat, they've misunderstood what's actually holding things together.

This is a particularly sharp word for how many of us approach work and career today. We tie identity, security, and self-worth to professional output. We measure ourselves by promotions, by how busy we are, by how early we start and how late we stay. Psalm 127 doesn't shame the hustle; it reframes it. The question isn't "how hard are you working" but "who are you ultimately trusting while you work?"

## What Trusting God with Your Work Looks Like

The line "for so he giveth his beloved sleep" (sometimes rendered "he grants sleep to those he loves") is the psalm's quiet punchline. Sleep here isn't just about rest after a long shift — it's a symbol of trust. The person who can actually close their eyes at night, rather than lying awake running through worst-case scenarios about their job, their finances, their future, is the person who has handed the outcome to God rather than gripping it with white knuckles.

Practically, this doesn't mean doing less at work or caring less about your career. It means examining why you're working the way you are. Are you striving because you genuinely can't sleep until the outcome is secured by your own hands alone? Or can you work diligently during the day and release the results at night, trusting that the house being built — your career, your family's provision, your future — ultimately rests in hands bigger than yours?

If work has become a source of constant anxiety, if your career has quietly become the thing you're trusting to secure your worth and your future, Psalm 127 is an invitation to rebuild on a different foundation. Keep showing up, keep doing good work — but let God be the one actually building the house.

PRAYER: Lord, I confess how often I treat my work and career like something only I can secure, staying up late and waking up anxious as if it all depends on me. Thank you for the reminder that unless you build the house, my labor is in vain no matter how hard I work. Teach me to work diligently without gripping the outcome so tightly. Give me the kind of trust that lets me actually rest at night. Build what only you can build, and let me partner with you instead of striving without you. In Jesus' name, amen.
