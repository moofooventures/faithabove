## A Command, Not a Suggestion

Moses had just died. The man who led Israel out of Egypt, who parted the sea, who spoke with God face to face, was gone, and leadership of an entire nation fell to Joshua — a man who had spent forty years as an assistant, never the one in charge. In front of him stood the Jordan River at flood stage and the fortified city of Jericho, the first obstacle in a land full of walled cities and armies far better equipped than Israel's. This is the moment God chooses to say "be strong and of a good courage."

Notice the grammar: "have not I commanded thee?" This is not God offering Joshua a pep talk or a suggestion he's free to take or leave. It's an imperative, repeated three times across this short chapter (verses 6, 7, and 9), because God knew Joshua would need to hear it more than once. Courage here isn't the absence of fear — it's a command given directly to someone who had every natural reason to be afraid. God doesn't tell Joshua his fear is irrational. He tells him to act in spite of it, because the command comes paired with a reason.

## The Reason Courage Is Possible

The second half of the verse is what makes the first half bearable: "for the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest." This isn't a vague spiritual sentiment. The Hebrew word for "whithersoever" literally covers every direction Joshua could walk — there was no geography, no battle, no city gate he would approach that fell outside the promise. God wasn't offering courage as a feeling to manufacture. He was offering His presence as the fact that made courage reasonable.

This matters because courage that's just self-generated willpower runs out. You can psych yourself up for a hard conversation or a scary diagnosis for a little while, but eventually the adrenaline fades and the fear creeps back. The courage Joshua was commanded to have wasn't rooted in his own confidence — it was rooted in a Person who had already proven faithful. Just a few verses earlier, God reminds Joshua of His promise to Moses. The courage command is built on a track record, not a blank check.

## Where You Need This Command Today

Most of us aren't crossing a literal river into literal battle, but we carry our own version of Jericho: the diagnosis we're dreading, the conversation we've been avoiding, the new role we feel unqualified for, the loss we have to walk into without a map. Courage, biblically, was never about feeling fearless. It was about moving forward anyway because of who goes with you.

This is worth sitting with if you've been waiting to feel brave before you act. Joshua likely didn't feel ready either — he'd spent four decades in someone else's shadow. God's command came before the feeling, not after it. Practically, that means you don't wait for courage to arrive like a mood before you take the next step; you take the step, trusting the presence that was promised, and the courage often catches up to meet you there.

If there's a Jericho in front of you right now — something that has you quietly dismayed, something you keep putting off because it feels too big — Joshua 1:9 isn't offering you a motivational slogan. It's repeating God's own command, paired with His own promise: you are not walking into this alone, wherever "this" leads you.
