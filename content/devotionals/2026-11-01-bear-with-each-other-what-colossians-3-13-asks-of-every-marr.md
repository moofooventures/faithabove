## A Word Paul Didn't Soften

Paul wrote Colossians from a Roman prison cell, which is worth sitting with before you read another word about relationships. He wasn't writing from a season of ease. The word he chose for "forbearing" is anechomai, a Greek term that literally means to hold oneself up under something, to bear its weight without collapsing. This isn't polite tolerance. It's the picture of two people standing under a load that would crush either one alone, choosing to keep standing there together.

That matters because so much relationship advice today sells compatibility as the goal — find the person who never irritates you, never disappoints you, never makes you wait. Paul assumes the opposite. He assumes you will have a quarrel. The phrase "if any man have a quarrel against any" isn't hypothetical; the Colossian church was full of real friction between real people, and Paul addresses it as a given, not an exception.

## Forbearance Before Forgiveness

Notice the order: forbearing, then forgiving. Forbearance is the daily, unglamorous work of not keeping score while the relationship is still unfolding — the patience that absorbs a sharp word, a missed anniversary, a broken promise, without immediately demanding resolution. Forgiveness comes after, when the account actually needs to be closed. A marriage, a friendship, a family needs both. Without forbearance, you'll never make it to the moment forgiveness is needed, because you'll have already walked out. Without forgiveness, forbearance curdles into quiet resentment.

The standard Paul gives is staggering: "even as Christ forgave you." Not as much as, not similar to — as Christ forgave you. That's not a standard you reach through willpower. It's a standard that only makes sense if you remember what you were actually forgiven for. Most conflict in a marriage or close relationship escalates because both people feel like the wronged party. Paul's answer isn't to settle who's right. It's to remember the cross settled something far larger first.

## What This Looks Like on a Tuesday

This verse rarely gets tested in dramatic moments. It gets tested when your spouse forgets, again, to do the thing they said they would. When a friend cancels for the third time. When someone you love says something careless in front of others. The temptation is to catalog it, to let it become evidence in a case you're quietly building. Forbearance says: not yet. Maybe not ever. Let this be absorbed rather than stored.

That doesn't mean relationships should be doormats for repeated harm — forbearance isn't the same as enabling, and some things genuinely need to be addressed directly and honestly. But most of what strains a marriage isn't betrayal; it's the slow accumulation of small, unforgiven frictions. Paul is naming the muscle that keeps those frictions from becoming fractures.

If you're in a hard season with someone you love, ask what it would look like to bear with them today instead of correcting them today. Not because the issue doesn't matter, but because timing and posture matter too. Love that lasts isn't love that never has reason to forgive. It's love that has practiced forgiving so often it no longer keeps a ledger.
