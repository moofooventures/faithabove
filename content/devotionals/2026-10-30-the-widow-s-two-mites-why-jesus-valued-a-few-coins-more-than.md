## A Scene in the Temple Courts

The temple treasury in Jerusalem had thirteen trumpet-shaped collection boxes, each one marked for a specific purpose — wood for the altar, incense, repairs, and so on. Wealthy worshippers would come with their gifts, and the custom of the day allowed for some public display; the clink of coins dropping into metal, the visible sign of a large donation, carried a kind of social status. Jesus sat near this treasury, watching, when a widow approached and dropped in two lepta — the smallest coins in circulation, together worth a fraction of a single day's wage. Mark tells us they were worth a "kodrantes," a Roman quadrans, practically nothing by any material measure.

Jesus didn't just notice her. He called his disciples over to make a point out of it. In a temple full of large, visible gifts, he directed their attention to the gift nobody else would have looked at twice.

## Generosity Measured by What's Left, Not What's Given

Jesus' math here defies accounting logic. He says she gave "more than all" the others, not metaphorically, but in a real moral calculation. The wealthy gave from their surplus — money they would never miss, money that cost them nothing in terms of daily living. The widow gave "of her want," meaning she gave the very money she needed to survive. Luke's parallel account specifies it was "all the living that she had."

This reframes what generosity actually measures. It isn't the size of the gift; it's the size of the sacrifice behind it. A person who gives a large sum out of abundance hasn't necessarily been more generous than someone who gives a small sum out of true need — generosity is about what the gift costs you, not what it's worth to someone else. This doesn't mean large gifts are meaningless; it means the heart behind the gift, and the trust it requires, is what God is actually weighing.

## Why This Widow Could Give Everything

It's worth asking what made this kind of generosity possible for her. A widow in first-century Judea had almost no financial safety net — no husband to provide, limited legal standing, often dependent on charity herself. Giving away her last two coins wasn't a calculated financial decision; it was an act of radical trust that God would provide for what came next. Her generosity wasn't rooted in having plenty. It was rooted in believing God was trustworthy even when her resources weren't.

That's the piece easy to miss if we only focus on the dollar amount. The widow's gift is impressive not because it was small, but because of what her smallness revealed about her faith. She gave as though God, not her remaining coins, was her actual security.

## What This Looks Like Today

Most of us will never face a decision as stark as hers, but the principle translates directly. Generosity isn't primarily about writing checks you'll barely notice missing from your account. It's about giving in a way that requires you to actually trust God with what comes next — whether that's money, time, or energy you feel you don't have to spare. It might look like showing up for a friend when you're exhausted, giving financially when your own budget feels tight, or offering your time to someone who can't repay it.

Jesus is still watching the treasury, in a sense. He still notices the gifts the world overlooks — not because they're large, but because of what they cost the person who gave them. The question worth sitting with today isn't "how much should I give?" but "does my giving require any trust in God at all?"
