## A Love Letter to a Fighting Church, Not a Wedding

It surprises a lot of people to learn that 1 Corinthians 13 was never written for a bride and groom. Paul wrote it to the church at Corinth — a congregation fractured by jealousy, favoritism, lawsuits, and spiritual one-upmanship. These believers were gifted, loud, and deeply divided. So when Paul describes love as patient and kind, not envious or easily provoked, he isn't offering poetry for a ceremony. He's confronting people who were actively failing to love one another well.

That context matters for how you read this passage today, especially in a marriage or any close relationship. The Greek word Paul uses, agape, isn't about butterflies or attraction. It's a decision, a posture, a daily action. 'Suffereth long' translates makrothymia — literally 'long-tempered,' the opposite of a short fuse. It's the choice to stay rather than snap, to stay rather than keep tally.

## The Record You Keep Without Noticing

Verse 5 ends with a phrase easy to skim past: love 'thinketh no evil.' The word Paul uses, logizetai, is an accounting term — it means to calculate, to keep a ledger. Paul is saying real love refuses to keep books on the people closest to us. No running total of who apologized less, who forgot the anniversary, who said the sharp word first.

Most relationship strain isn't caused by one explosive betrayal. It's caused by a thousand small entries quietly added to a ledger neither person admits to keeping. You remember the time they were short with you at dinner. They remember the time you took a call during the moment that mattered. Neither of you says anything, and the account grows.

## What This Looks Like on an Ordinary Tuesday

Paul's picture of love isn't dramatic — it's patient, kind, not self-seeking, not easily provoked. That's slow, unglamorous work. It looks like choosing not to interrupt with a correction when your spouse tells a story slightly wrong. It looks like asking how their day actually was and listening past the first sentence. It looks like letting an irritation pass without turning it into evidence.

If you're in a marriage, this passage is a mirror more than a decoration. Read it replacing the word 'charity' with your own name: is Sarah patient? Is Sarah kind? Does Sarah keep score? That exercise humbles fast, and that's the point. Paul wasn't handing Corinth — or us — a standard to admire from a distance. He was describing the kind of love only possible when we draw on a source bigger than our own patience: the love God has already poured into us.

Close the ledger today. Not because the other person has earned it, but because that's what this love actually does.
